Directed-Network Conflation by Dynamic Time Warping on Graphs: Route-Consistent Map-to-Map Matching under Divergent Segmentation

Kaveh · Sonocount Research
network-matching library · working paper
Abstract

Conflation — the problem of deciding which features of one geographic map correspond to which features of another — is a prerequisite for fusing attributes across independently maintained road datasets (e.g. transferring traffic counts from a national road register onto an OpenStreetMap network). We address the directed, route-valued form of the problem: given two directed road networks A and B that model the same physical streets but are segmented differently, assign to each directed edge of A a connected, direction-respecting route of B-edges that minimises geometric shape drift while remaining junction-consistent — edges of A that meet at a junction must agree on where that junction lands in B. Classical curve-alignment and GPS-trajectory map-matching methods do not fit: the former align two open curves, not a curve to a directed graph with branches and cycles, and the latter match a noisy point sequence to a network but assume a single moving agent rather than a whole branching sub-map that must be labelled coherently. We develop a family of alignment algorithms built on Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) (Sakoe & Chiba, 1978; Berndt & Clifford, 1994). Graph-DTW warps a single directed A-edge against a local directed target graph GB that may contain cycles, replacing DTW's scalar predecessor with a within-row Dijkstra (Dijkstra, 1959) over GB's arcs; it supports a point emission and a segment emission with a bearing term that pins heading. DAG-DTW generalises the source from a single path to a whole acyclic branching neighbourhood GA, swept once in topological order (Kahn, 1962), with a split-factor recurrence that conserves cost-flow across branches and a forward–backward pass that scores each junction once for every route through it. For genuine reconvergences (diamonds) we provide two exact reference solvers over a shared min-sum belief-propagation forest base (Pearl, 1988), which cross-validate to a common optimum (238/238 checks) and reveal that the residual error on a shifted diamond is a point-mode drift limit — the nearest-versus-corresponding problem — rather than a loop-labelling failure, motivating the bearing-aware objective as the principled fix. We formalise the problem, the objective, and the notion of junction consistency used throughout the paper.

1 Introduction

Maintaining more than one digital model of the same road system is the normal state of affairs, not the exception. A national road-attribute register, a crowd-sourced street map, a commercial navigation base, and a municipality's asset inventory each describe the same physical streets, but they are digitised independently: they cut a road into edges at different places, place junction nodes at slightly different coordinates, and disagree on geometry by a metre or more. To combine their attributes — to carry a per-minute traffic flow measured on the register's segmentation onto the crowd-sourced network's segmentation, say — one must first solve conflation: establish which feature of one map corresponds to which feature of the other (Newson & Krumm, 2009, frame the closely related applied setting).

For road networks the correspondence is neither one-to-one nor symmetric between features. Because the two maps are segmented differently, a single edge of the source map A typically spans several consecutive edges of the target map B, and conversely a long B-edge may absorb several short A-edges. The correct output is therefore not a node-to-node or edge-to-edge assignment but a route assignment: each directed A-edge is mapped to a connected, directed path of B-edges that it traverses. Three properties make this hard.

The networks are directed. A road carries a legal and geometric direction; a two-way street is modelled as two opposing directed edges (in the Swedish NVDB register, for example, as is_reverse twins). A correct matcher must never let a route run backward along a B-edge, and must never let a source path that happens to run against the flow of a nearby B-edge "borrow" that edge instead of its reverse twin. Undirected or symmetric shape-matching silently permits both.

The segmentation is divergent. Neither map's cut points are canonical. The alignment must be free to let one A-vertex cover a run of several B-vertices (when B is sampled more densely) and to let several A-vertices share one stretch of B — exactly the many-to-one warping that DTW was designed for, but here the "time axis" on the target side is a graph, with branches and cycles (roundabouts, divided-carriageway loops), not a line.

Junctions must stay consistent. Where several A-edges meet at a junction, they share a single physical point; the matching must place that shared point at a single location in B. If each A-edge is matched to its own individually-cheapest B-route, the routes generically disagree about where the junction lands — they collide at the junction and one of them is forced into a backward or disconnected step. Junction consistency cannot, in general, be assembled from per-edge-optimal pieces; it is a joint constraint over all edges incident to the junction.

Why existing methods do not fit. Two mature bodies of work are adjacent but miss the target.

Curve-to-curve alignment. DTW (Sakoe & Chiba, 1978; Berndt & Clifford, 1994) and the Fréchet/edit-distance family align two open, linearly-ordered curves. A road edge is such a curve, but a road neighbourhood is a directed graph: it branches, it merges, and it can contain directed cycles. There is no single monotone warping axis to align against, and no notion of a junction shared by several curves. Applying curve alignment edge-by-edge throws away precisely the junction-consistency coupling that makes conflation hard.

GPS-trajectory map-matching. HMM-style map-matching (Newson & Krumm, 2009) snaps a noisy, time-ordered sequence of points from a single moving vehicle onto a road network. It shares our directed-graph target and our shortest-path transitions, but its input is one thread, not a branching sub-map, and it has no obligation to keep several threads consistent where they cross. It answers "which road was this one trajectory on?", not "which route of B does each edge of a whole branching piece of A correspond to, such that they all agree at the junctions?".

Our approach keeps DTW's warping intuition but replaces both of its linear axes with graphs. On the target side, the horizontal (target-advances) move becomes a Dijkstra shortest-path relaxation over GB's arcs, so a single A-vertex may ride a run of B-vertices and the target may cycle. On the source side, we generalise from a single directed path to a directed acyclic neighbourhood GA swept in topological order, with a split-factor recurrence that (i) sums — not minimises — over the branches entering a junction so that every incident edge is covered, and (ii) divides each vertex's accumulated cost equally among its outgoing edges so that each vertex's local cost is counted exactly once across all routes, for any DAG shape. A forward–backward pass then scores each junction once, jointly, for all routes through it. Reconvergences (diamonds) are the one place message passing on a tree is not exact; we characterise this case precisely with two exact reference solvers and show its residual error is a property of the point emission, correctable by a direction-aware (bearing) emission rather than by more loop machinery.

1.1 Contributions

  • Graph-DTW: DTW against a directed graph target. A dynamic program that warps one directed A-edge onto a local target graph GB that may contain directed cycles, by replacing DTW's scalar predecessor with a within-row Dijkstra over GB's arcs (so one A-vertex can cover a run of B-vertices and GB can loop). It admits a point emission and a segment emission (middle-to-middle distance plus a λ-weighted bearing term) that pins heading and forbids backward traversal by construction.
  • A projection-based, continuous local-graph builder. A single construction turns both sides into local directed graphs whose vertex pools are enriched by the foot-of-perpendicular projections of the other side's nodes (the projection-pool idea of Keogh & Ratanamahatana, 2005, applied to a graph target), keeps every vertex owned by exactly one edge, and stitches coincident junction endpoints with free directed inter-edge arcs — the structural rule that prevents route collapse and topological U-turns.
  • DAG-DTW: generalising the source from a path to a DAG. A joint solve over a whole acyclic source neighbourhood GA, swept once in topological order (Kahn, 1962), with a coverage sum over incoming branches and a 1/outdeg split factor that yields a conserved cost-flow (each vertex's emission counted exactly once across all sinks, diamonds included). Graph-DTW is the single-path special case; the recurrence reduces to DTW's three moves on a chain.
  • Forward–backward junction consistency. A backward table on the reversed pair of graphs (with the symmetric 1/indeg split) and a reachability-guarded backtrack that pins every junction by φ(j) = argminv ( D[j][v] + B[j][v] − E(j,v) ) subject to forward-reaching all fixed successors. On trees/polytrees this is exact and eliminates backward steps; require_tree asserts that regime.
  • Two exact conditioning solvers as validation. A recursive minimum-vertex-cut solver (linear in the number of loops) and a one-shot minimum-feedback-vertex-set solver (exponential), over a shared exact min-sum belief-propagation forest base (Pearl, 1988). They minimise the same realized objective and provably agree; the mutual cross-check (238/238) both validates correctness and exposed two real solver bugs.
  • An honest characterisation of the reconvergence limit. We show that the residual error on a shifted diamond is the nearest-versus-corresponding drift limit of the point emission — confirmed to be the true point-mode optimum by the exact solvers — not a loop-conditioning failure, and therefore that the principled fix is the direction (bearing) term, positioning segment-mode DAG-DTW as future work rather than more loop machinery.
  • Practical controls. An α (horizontal_weight) knob that discounts the emission on 1:N coverage without laundering carried cost; an arc-length re-match that places positions jump-free along each route; and a downstream-ready routes_detail output with directed, partial-coverage fractions.

2 Problem Formulation

Networks. Let A and B be directed road networks modelling the same physical streets. Each is a set of directed edges; a two-way road appears as two opposing directed edges. Every edge carries a polyline geometry, and edges meet at shared junction points. We call A the source (the network to be matched) and B the target.

Local graphs and sampling. Conflation is solved locally, on a neighbourhood at a time. From A we extract a connected source sub-network and represent it as a local directed graph GA whose vertices are geometry samples and whose arcs are the directed sample-to-sample steps within each edge, plus free directed inter-edge arcs stitching coincident junction endpoints (edge u's end → edge w's start). GB is the analogous local graph on the target. Each edge is sampled every Δ metres (step_meters) into vertices; a vertex belongs to exactly one edge, recorded by vert_edge(v). For a source vertex a we write Apred(a) for its predecessors in GA and outdeg(a) for its out-degree; for a target vertex v we write Bpred(v) for its predecessors in GB.

The two local graphs differ in an essential, deliberately-exploited asymmetry:

  • GA (source) is required to be a directed acyclic graph — a topologically-orderable branching neighbourhood (a single directed path in the graph-DTW special case). A cyclic source component is out of scope for the joint solver.
  • GB (target) may contain directed cycles (roundabouts, divided-carriageway loops). No acyclicity is assumed on the target.

Emission (local cost). The local cost of aligning source vertex a to target vertex v is E(a,v) ≥ 0. In point mode E(a,v) = ‖a − v‖, the Euclidean distance in metres. In segment mode the state is an (A-segment, B-arc) pair (i,e) with e = u→v a forward B-arc, and

(1) E(i,e) = ‖mid(ai,ai+1) − mid(u,v)‖ + λ · dcirc( θ(si), θ(e) ),

the middle-to-middle distance between the source segment si and the target arc plus λ = bearing_weight times the circular heading difference dcirc ∈ [0°, 180°]. The bearing term is what pins heading, since a middle-to-middle distance alone is blind to a segment rotating about its own middle.

Output: a route assignment. A solution is a label map φ from source vertices to target vertices, from which each source edge's matched route — the connected, direction-respecting sequence of B-edges it traverses — is read off by grouping consecutive labels by vert_edge. Equivalently, the solution assigns to each directed A-edge a connected path of B-edges, oriented consistently with the A-edge's direction. Because GB's arcs are forward-only and inter-edge stitches are non-ridable connectivity, any φ produced is directed and cannot U-turn onto an edge's reverse twin.

Constraints. A valid solution must satisfy:

  1. Directed connectivity. For every source arc a→a′, the target label must advance forward: φ(a′) is forward-reachable from φ(a) in GB. Consecutive B-edges of a route are graph-connected. No backward or teleporting step is allowed.
  2. Full coverage. Every source edge is assigned a route; no incident edge at a junction may be dropped. This is the reason the branch operator at a merge is a sum, not a min, over Apred.
  3. Junction consistency. All source vertices coincident at a physical junction j must receive a single, mutually-agreeing target label φ(j), so that every route passing through j crosses B at the same place. Formally, the routes incident to j share the target position φ(j); a matching that forces different incident routes to disagree at j is inconsistent.

In the shipped point-mode matcher, junction consistency is enforced as region consistency: coincident source vertices at a junction land within one sample step Δ of a common target location. Vertex-exact junction resolution is what the exact conditioning solvers add.

Objective. Among valid solutions, minimise total shape drift, the realized emission summed over the final, junction-consistent labels:

(2) Ctotal = Σa E( a, φ(a) )

evaluated on the final consistent φ (this quantity is well-defined and correct for any graph shape). Reported quality is the average drift Ctotal / (matched steps) in metres, together with per-edge drift and coverage. Ctotal is count-weighted and hence depends on the sampling density Δ; a length-weighted, density-independent objective is left to future work.

We stress a correctness caveat used throughout the paper: the dynamic program's own optimum, Σsinks t minv D[t][v] (where D is the forward cost table introduced in the next section), coincides with Ctotal only when GA is a tree. On a reconvergent diamond it is neither the realized cost nor a bound on it. The realized sum Ctotal over the final labels is the definition of solution quality; the DP optimum is a diagnostic, not the objective.

Junction consistency vs. per-edge optimality (the crux). Consider two routes s1→t1 and s2→t2 through a common junction j. Optimised independently, route 1 may prefer j at one target vertex and route 2 at another; but j carries a single label φ(j). Forcing one route's preference onto j pushes the other into a backward step, even when every A-edge is matched to the geometrically correct B-edge. Hence the objective above cannot be minimised edge-by-edge: junction consistency couples all edges incident to j, and a joint solve over the source neighbourhood is required. The mechanism by which the algorithms of this paper enforce that coupling — the split-factor recurrence, the forward–backward junction score, and (for reconvergences) the exact conditioning solvers — is the subject of the sections that follow.

3 Related Work

Our method sits at the intersection of four largely separate literatures: (i) probabilistic matching of a GPS trajectory to a road map, (ii) geometric curve matching under the Fréchet distance, (iii) dynamic time warping (DTW) for elastic sequence alignment, and (iv) map conflation / multi-representation matching in GIS. It also borrows inference machinery from graphical models. We review each family, then state precisely how network-to-network, directed, junction-consistent, segmentation-robust shape conflation differs from all of them. Throughout, A denotes the source network and B the target, both directed (Section 2).

3.1 GPS-trajectory-to-map matching

The dominant paradigm for online navigation and floating-car data is matching a noisy point sequence (a GPS track) to the edges of a known road graph. Early geometric and topological methods score candidate edges by proximity and heading and grow a matched path incrementally (White et al., 2000); the survey of Quddus et al. (2007) catalogues this generation. The field then consolidated around a hidden Markov model (HMM): each GPS sample is an observation, each nearby road edge a hidden state, emission probabilities encode measurement noise, transition probabilities encode routing plausibility (great-circle vs. on-road distance), and the Viterbi algorithm recovers the maximum-likelihood edge sequence (Newson & Krumm, 2009). This framing is robust to noise and sparse sampling and is now standard.

The structural difference from our setting is the source. In HMM map matching the source is an unstructured point cloud with no topology of its own: it never branches, never merges, and imposes no junction constraints. The hidden chain is a path, so Viterbi — a one-dimensional DP — suffices. In network-to-network conflation the source A is itself a directed graph with its own vertices, edges, out-/in-degrees and junctions, and the requirement is that source edges meeting at a junction agree on where that junction lands in B. That is a constraint across several simultaneously-matched paths, not along one. Our forward–backward tables and joint junction argmin (Section 5) generalise the Viterbi idea from a chain to a DAG source, and our horizontal Dijkstra move over GB generalises the transition step to absorb the different segmentation between A and B (one A-vertex may ride a run of B-vertices) rather than treating each edge as a single discrete state.

3.2 Curve and map matching via the Fréchet distance

A second family measures the similarity of two polylines directly. The Fréchet distance — the classic "dog-walking" metric — and the free-space diagram of Alt & Godau (1995) give an exact, order-preserving notion of curve similarity and an O(mn log mn) decision procedure. Brakatsoulas et al. (2005) apply both the Fréchet and a weaker average Fréchet distance to match a vehicle trajectory against road-network polylines, making the geometry (not a probabilistic emission) the primary signal.

This family is the closest in spirit to ours: it is shape-first and reparametrisation-invariant, exactly the property needed when A and B sample the same physical road at different vertex densities. But the standard formulation matches one curve to one curve (or to individually-enumerated network paths); it is undirected in its basic form, carries no explicit heading term, and offers no mechanism for a shared junction label when several curves branch off a common point. Our emission is a DTW-style alignment cost rather than a bottleneck (max) distance, which lets us (a) sum a total shape-drift objective Ctotal = Σa E(a, φ(a)) that is comparable across edges and (b) add the middle-to-middle + λ·Δbearing segment cost that pins direction — the term that distinguishes a road from its anti-parallel twin. Where Fréchet matching answers "how far apart are these two curves," we answer "which connected, directed route of B-edges does each A-edge correspond to, consistently at junctions."

3.3 Dynamic time warping

The elastic-alignment engine we build on is DTW, introduced for spoken-word recognition by Sakoe & Chiba (1978), who also gave the band constraint we reuse to restrict B-candidates (Section 2). Berndt & Clifford (1994) established DTW as a general time-series pattern-matching tool. Classical DTW aligns two 1-D sequences on a rectangular cost grid with three moves (vertical / horizontal / diagonal). Our contribution is to keep the three-move recurrence but replace one sequence axis with a directed graph: the horizontal move becomes a Dijkstra shortest path over GB (whose arcs may form cycles, e.g. roundabouts), and — in DAG-DTW — the other axis is generalised from a path to a topologically-ordered source DAG with a 1/outdeg split factor that conserves cost-flow across branches. To our knowledge, DTW with a directed-graph alignment target, swept jointly over a branching source, is the novel core of this work. The continuous projection-pool construction of the local graphs follows the "projection" refinement of DTW popularised by Keogh and Ratanamahatana (2004), adapted here to a graph target so that every foot-of-perpendicular projection of one network's nodes onto the other becomes an alignable vertex.

3.4 Map conflation and multi-representation matching

The GIS conflation literature is where our problem — reconciling two independently-digitised maps of the same area — originates, going back to Saalfeld's (1988) formalisation of automated map compilation as node/arc correspondence with iterative rubber-sheeting. A large body of work matches street datasets across representations using buffer-growing, node-degree and topological signatures, and geometric attributes, often iteratively (Volz, 2006). These methods are explicitly network-to-network and address different segmentation, but they typically operate on undirected feature geometry, decide correspondence with buffer-overlap or node-matching heuristics rather than an optimal shape-alignment DP, and enforce junction agreement only indirectly (through node matching), without a single joint objective. Our method contributes an optimisation view of the same task: a shape-drift DP whose optimum is the matched route set, that is directed by construction (a source edge running against a target edge matches that target's reverse twin, never U-turning), and whose junctions are resolved jointly by a forward–backward score rather than by independent per-node matches.

3.5 Inference tools we borrow

For junction consistency we adopt standard graphical-model inference. The forward–backward tables and the joint junction argmin φ(j) = argminv( D[j][v] + B[j][v] − E(j, v) ) are min-sum (max-product) belief propagation, which is exact on trees/polytrees (Pearl, 1988); this is the same principle underlying Viterbi decoding. For genuine reconvergent loops (the "diamond," where two directed paths from a common ancestor re-meet and the graph carries an undirected cycle), we fall back — in our exact reference solvers — to cutset conditioning: fix a small feedback-vertex / separator set, solve the now-acyclic remainder exactly, and combine, in the spirit of Dechter's (1990) cutset decomposition for constraint and probabilistic networks. We stress the borrowed-machinery framing honestly: BP is exact only because the underlying forest solver is; on a true loop it is our conditioning solvers, not plain message passing, that certify optimality (Section 6). These conditioning solvers are validated reference implementations and are not wired into the shipped matcher, which uses a reachability-guarded backtrack that is exact on trees.

4 Graph-DTW: dynamic time warping to a directed graph

4.1 From a scalar time axis to a directed graph

Classical dynamic time warping (DTW) aligns two sequences by a monotone warping path through a cost grid, using three local moves — advance the first sequence, advance the second, or advance both (Sakoe & Chiba, 1978; Berndt & Clifford, 1994). Its second axis is a total order: at step j the only predecessor is j−1. Graph-DTW retains DTW's move set but replaces that scalar axis with a directed target graph GB, so that the predecessor of a B-vertex v is any graph-predecessor u ∈ pred(v). The source remains a single directed path: one A-edge, densified into vertices a0,…,aN−1 at spacing Δ (step_meters). Both sides are built by the same projection-based, continuous builder (build_local_digraph), which augments each edge's vertex pool with the feet of perpendicular projections of the opposite side's nodes and gap-fills evenly, following the projection-pool refinement of DTW (Keogh & Ratanamahatana, 2005) transported to a graph target; every vertex is owned by exactly one edge, and coincident junction endpoints are kept as separate coincident vertices stitched by directed inter-edge arcs. The construction is detailed in Appendix A.

Let D[i][v] denote the minimum cost to align the source prefix a0…ai so that ai is labelled by B-vertex v. With the point-mode emission E(a,v) = ‖a−v‖, the recurrence is DTW's three moves with j−1 generalised to pred(v):

(3) D[i][v] = E(ai,v) + min( D[i−1][v] (vertical), minu∈pred(v)D[i][u] (horizontal), minu∈pred(v)D[i−1][u] (diagonal) )

The vertical move advances A while holding the B-position (source denser than target); the diagonal advances both; the horizontal advances B while holding ai (target denser than source, so one A-vertex rides a run of B-vertices). Entry is free, D[0][v] = E(a0,v) for every v, and termination takes minv D[N−1][v]: the warping path always spans the entire A-edge, but is free to begin and end anywhere in GB.

4.2 The horizontal move is a Dijkstra pass

The one structural subtlety is that the horizontal term self-references D[i][·] within the same row i. In classical DTW this is harmless — the second axis is acyclic, so a left-to-right sweep suffices. Here GB models real road topology and may contain directed cycles (roundabouts, loops), so no topological sweep of row i exists. Because every emission is non-negative, however, the within-row problem is exactly a non-negative-weight shortest-path problem and is solved exactly by Dijkstra's algorithm (Dijkstra, 1959) over the arcs of GB. Concretely, first form the row base from the two moves that read the finalised row i−1,

(4) base[v] = E(ai,v) + min( D[i−1][v], minu∈pred(v)D[i−1][u] )

then run Dijkstra, relaxing along each arc u→w of GB and re-paying the emission on arrival:

(5) D[i][w] ← min( D[i][w], D[i][u] + E(ai,w) )

Re-paying E(ai,w) on every relaxation is what lets a single A-vertex ride an arbitrarily long run of B-vertices at its own local cost, and keeps every horizontal edge weight non-negative so Dijkstra remains exact on a cyclic GB. The cost per A-edge is O(N · |EB| log |VB|); the local graphs are small.

4.3 Backtracking to a route

Backtracking the argmins of (1) yields a vertex-to-vertex label map φ; grouping consecutive steps by their owning B-edge (vert_edge[v]) turns it into an ordered route of B-edges. Because the builder emits only forward arcs — a bidirectional road is already two opposing directed edges, and inter-edge junction stitches are sub-half-metre free connectors that can never host a DP state — the recovered route is directed and connected by construction, and can neither U-turn onto a side edge nor hop onto a geometrically close but topologically disconnected parallel road. Leading and trailing zero-traversal touches, where the source merely grazes a junction vertex without walking the edge, are trimmed; if nothing is traversed the edge returns NO_MATCH.

4.4 Segment and bearing emission

Point mode scores a vertex against a vertex and is blind to orientation. The segment emission (_segment_dp_pairs) instead lifts the DP state to a pair (A-segment, B-arc): state (i,e) means "A-segment si = ai→ai+1 rides forward B-arc e = u→v". Its per-state cost is a middle-to-middle distance plus an optional heading penalty,

(6) E(i,e) = ‖mid(ai,ai+1) − mid(u,v)‖ + λ · dcirc( θ(si), θ(e) )

where θ(·) is a heading, dcirc ∈ [0°,180°] is the circular heading difference, and λ = bearing_weight. The three moves carry over unchanged — A-advance on the same arc (N:1), arc-advance within a row via the same Dijkstra (1:N), or both — and the arc-state optimum is emitted back as vertex pairs, so all downstream grouping, trimming and metrics are shared with point mode. The bearing term is necessary because a middle-to-middle distance alone is invariant to a segment rotating about its own midpoint; λ pins heading, with λ ∈ [1,5] a good working range. Two earlier emission variants (endpoint-average distance, and bearing charged only on diagonal moves) were superseded by (4); emission="midpoint" is a deprecated alias for "segment".

5 DAG-DTW: generalising the source to a DAG

5.1 Motivation and the design asymmetry

Graph-DTW matches one A-edge at a time. But road networks branch, and A-edges meeting at a junction should agree on where that junction lands in B. DAG-DTW therefore promotes the source from a single path to a whole connected acyclic subgraph GA — a junction neighbourhood or branching corridor — matched in one joint solve (match_dag_to_bgraph). The design rests on a deliberate asymmetry between the two sides:

  • The source GA must be a DAG. It is swept once in topological order (Kahn, 1962); a cyclic local component is rejected (NotADAG). Acyclicity is what makes a single non-iterative sweep exact: when a vertex is reached, all its source-predecessors are already finalised.
  • The target GB may cycle. As in graph-DTW, the horizontal move over GB remains a Dijkstra pass, so roundabouts and loops in the target are handled without change.

The topological order is laid out as three blocks — [all sources][middle][all sinks] — matching the DP's three phases: free entry at sources, propagation through the middle, free exit at sinks.

5.2 The coverage recurrence and the split factor

Let D[a][v] be the minimum cost to align the source down to A-vertex a, ending at B-vertex v, with every A-edge above a covered. That coverage clause — not "a shortest path through the DAG" — dictates the operators:

(7) D[a][v] = E(a,v) + min( minv′∈Bpred(v)D[a][v′] (H) B advances, Σa′∈Apred(a) 1outdeg(a′) minv′∈Bpred(v)∪{v}D[a′][v′] (A) A advances )

Two DAG-specific features sit on the (A) branch:

Sum over source-predecessors (coverage). At a merge, the costs of all incoming A-edges are added, not minimised: every approach must be aligned, none discarded. The rule is sum over branches, min over B-positions; a min in place of the sum would leave one branch unmatched.

The 1/outdeg(a′) split factor (conserved cost-flow). Each vertex divides its accumulated cost equally among its outgoing A-edges. This makes each vertex's emission E contribute exactly once when summed across all sinks, for any DAG shape, including reconvergent ones. Chain and merge vertices have outdeg = 1 (factor 1); only splits divide. Appendix B gives the conservation proof; §8.2 gives a worked Y-split.

5.3 Reductions and computation

The recurrence (5) contains graph-DTW as a special case, with no branch logic:

  • Chain (|Apred(a)| = 1, outdeg = 1): the split factor is 1 and the sum has one term, collapsing (5) to E(a,v) + min( D[a][v′], D[a′][v], D[a′][v′] ) — exactly graph-DTW's three moves (1).
  • Source (Apred(a) = ∅): the empty sum is 0, so D[a][v] = E(a,v) is seeded and the (H) Dijkstra then spreads it — free entry at every source. Sinks are read off with a free minv: free exit.

Computation (_dp_table) is one topological sweep of GA. Per A-vertex, seed

(8) seed[v] = E(a,v) + Σa′∈Apred(a) D[a′][v]outdeg(a′)

then run the within-a Dijkstra over GB (the diagonal v′ ∈ Bpred(v) is absorbed as the first relaxation). No iteration to convergence is needed: acyclicity of GA guarantees every Apred(a) is finalised when a is reached. This is precisely graph-DTW's structure with "row i" replaced by "A-vertex a in topological order" and the single-row seed replaced by the split-scaled sum over incoming A-branches. DAG-DTW is thus a strict superset of graph-DTW: a single-path source is graph-DTW. The recurrence is implemented in point mode ([IMPL]); the segment/bearing lift of §4.4 into the DAG recurrence is future work (§7.4).

6 Junction consistency

6.1 Why per-junction argmin fails

Solving each sink's route independently and then stitching the pieces does not yield a consistent global matching. Consider two routes s1→t1 and s2→t2 that both pass through a junction j. Optimised in isolation, route 1 may want j at one B-vertex and route 2 at another — but j carries a single label φ(j). A greedy per-sink backtrack traces both routes back and they collide at j; forcing one label onto the other route creates a non-forward B-transition on a GA arc — a backward step. Crucially this occurs even when every A-edge maps to the correct B-edge: the error is in the shared junction label, not the topology. One cannot assemble the whole matching from each-sink's-own-best pieces.

6.2 Forward–backward resolution

The fix scores each junction once, for every route through it, using two cost tables built by the same _dp_table:

  • Forward D[a][v] (§5): cheapest cost to align sources → a with a at v, using the 1/outdeg split.
  • Backward B[a][v]: reverse both graphs — GA's sinks become sources and GB's arcs flip — and run the same DP with the symmetric 1/indeg split, so the conserved cost-flow holds in both directions. This is the cheapest cost to align a → sinks with a at v.

The best whole-DAG cost among all matchings that keep j at v then pins each junction jointly:

(9) φ(j) = argminv ( D[j][v] + B[j][v] − E(j,v) )

where −E(j,v) removes the double count of j's own local cost, which appears in both tables. Equation (7) is a min-sum (max-product) belief-propagation reading, exact on trees (Pearl, 1988).

6.3 The reachability-guarded backtrack

The shipped procedure (dag_dtw.py) does not read (7) pointwise; it enforces joint consistency during a reverse-topological backtrack. At each non-sink a it scans v in increasing joint score D[a][v] + B[a][v] − E(a,v) and takes the cheapest v that still forward-reaches every already-fixed successor's label φ, tested by BFS reachability in GB; sinks take the free argmin. The reachability constraint is a cheap enforcement of consistency: it discards exactly the inconsistent tie-breaks that a rigid pointwise argmin would introduce. Dropping the constraint (a pure per-vertex argmin of D+B−E) regresses merge and y_split under perturbation.

6.4 Tree-exactness

When GA is a tree or polytree — branches never rejoin, the common case for an oriented junction neighbourhood — forward–backward with the reachability guard is exact. Over a 512-configuration perturbation sweep the backward step is eliminated on chain, merge, and y_split; only the reconvergent diamond still fails (§7). The require_tree=True flag asserts this regime by testing the undirected skeleton's cyclomatic number |E|−|V|+C = 0 (interior degree-2 samples never add a cycle, so a diamond is detected at any sampling density), raising NotATree on any reconvergence and thereby guaranteeing the exact regime for callers who know their source branches without rejoining. In the shipped point-mode matcher junction consistency is region-consistency: coincident A-vertices at a junction land within one sample step of a common B-location (measured spread 0.00 m on all clean synthetic scenarios). Vertex-exact resolution is what the conditioning solvers of §7.3 add.

7 Reconvergence and the direction term

7.1 The diamond

An undirected cycle in a DAG occurs exactly where two directed paths from a common ancestor re-meet: a diamond, split j1 → {up, down} → merge j2. On such a cycle the two sides share both endpoints. Forward–backward lets each side choose its own label where they re-meet — B[j1][v] sums the two branches as if each could independently pick j2 — so they can disagree, producing a backward step. The reachability guard of §6.3 cannot repair this, because the loop constrains j1 and j2 simultaneously in both directions; message passing is exact only on graphs with no undirected cycle (Pearl, 1988).

Two identities the paper must therefore not misuse. The realised total Ctotal = Σa E(a,φ(a)) from the final labels is always correct. But the conserved-flow reading Σsinks t minv D[t][v] (reported as dp_cost) and the junction quantity D[j][φ] + B[j][φ] − E are exact only on a tree. On a reconvergent diamond, D and B each conserve flow on their own, but combining them at one junction is weighted by both splits, which no longer cancel: on a perturbed diamond D+B−E at the split reads 112.8 while Σsinks min D = 96.9, whereas on every tree they agree exactly. Hence dp_cost is a DP diagnostic, not a bound on realised cost in either direction (§8.2).

7.2 The nearest-vs-corresponding limit

The honest diagnosis is that the diamond's failure is not a loop-labelling failure. Pin the split j1 to the exact B-split and solve the two branches (§7.3): in point mode, both A-branches still collapse onto the nearer B-edge, because that genuinely costs less drift — the correct one-to-one split never attains the minimum at any cut label. This is a nearest-vs-corresponding drift limit intrinsic to point mode, not a reconvergence artefact. Empirically, on the synthetic diamond the routing stays correct under rigid shifts up to 6 m and collapses only at 8 m (four sequence-rule violations), where the collapse is the true minimum-drift point-mode optimum. The cure is an emission that rewards heading agreement — the segment/bearing term of (4) — not more loop machinery (§7.4).

7.3 Exact conditioning as a reference solver

To confirm the above diagnosis rather than assume it, the repository ships two independent exact conditioning solvers (dag_conditioning.py), used as reference and validation and deliberately not wired into the production matcher ([REF]). Both minimise the same realised cost Σa E(a,φ(a)) on the same exact base, so they must return equal-cost labellings on any DAG — a mutual cross-check.

Shared exact base — min-sum belief propagation on the polytree (_forest_solve). The unary potential is drift plus folded pinned-boundary reachability constraints; the pairwise potential is the directed reachability constraint φ(head) ∈ Reach(φ(tail)); the solver is exact on any tree and returns +∞ (via the BP value itself, not a re-summed drift) for an infeasible pinning. Conditioning is exact only because this base is: an earlier heuristic base (the §6.3 backtrack) made the two solvers disagree on double_diamond (29.09 vs 29.33) before exact BP replaced it and both agreed at 28.93.

Solver 1 — recursive minimum-vertex-cut (_solve_recursive): if the free part is a forest, run BP; otherwise pick a small vertex cut S (usually one junction), enumerate its K-nearest B-candidates, pin, and recurse into the now-independent components. Cost (#cand)w with w the largest single cut (usually 1) — linear in the number of loops.

Solver 2 — one-shot minimum feedback-vertex-set (_solve_fvs): enumerate all label combinations of the whole set F jointly and solve the forest GA−F for each. Cost (#cand)|F| — exponential. The FVS characterises completion (F = ∅ ⇔ tree ⇔ shipped solver already exact) but is a poor way to label.

Minimum cut and FVS are NP-hard in general (Karp, 1972), but local junction graphs are tiny, so a greedy separator suffices. The cross-validation is comprehensive: 238/238 checks pass, with diamond recursive = fvs = 24.581 and double_diamond (|F| = 2, the case that separates the two methods) = 28.930 for both — below either heuristic value — and BP matching independent brute-force enumeration on tiny cases. The mutual-agreement harness itself localised two real correctness bugs, both triggered by anti-parallel B geometry (normal in real conflation): infeasibility mis-scored as cheap-and-valid, and backward arcs internal to the pinned set left unchecked (Appendix C). The honest lesson: the labels are the hard part; conditioning buys exactness only on top of an exact forest solver.

7.4 The direction term as the fix

Because the diamond collapse is a point-mode drift phenomenon (§7.2), the principled remedy is to carry the segment/bearing emission (4) into the DAG recurrence, so that a branch running against the flow of the nearer B-edge pays a heading penalty and the corresponding split re-attains the minimum. Lifting the DAG recurrence to segment+bearing states is [DESIGN] future work; the shipped point-mode matcher retains the §6.3 reachability-guarded backtrack, with the conditioning solvers standing alongside as reference and validation rather than as production components.

8 Extraction, cost, and coverage weight

8.1 Arc-length re-match

The DP and backtrack decide topology — which B-edges each A-edge maps to; a final pass decides position. Pure point-to-point placement picks the nearest B-vertex per A-vertex, which under a large lateral offset compresses A onto part of a B-edge and produces a jump at the junction: coincident A-vertices land far apart in B, graph-reachable but discontinuous. Instead, each A-vertex is placed at its arc-length fraction between an entry and exit point on its route's B-polyline (snapped to the nearest route vertex). For an A-vertex at fractional arc-length s along its edge (_arclength_rematch),

(10) target(vi) = entry + (exit − entry) · si − sminsmax − smin

so the B-position advances proportionally to A and the match is jump-free. Entry and exit are free at a DAG source/sink (the endpoint projects onto the route and may land mid-edge) and pinned only at an interior junction. A boundary-yield rule handles a shared boundary B-edge when consecutive A-edges over- or undershoot a junction: the edge is awarded to whichever A-edge covers it with more vertices, else the junction-end pins to the far end and reintroduces a backward step. A known residual: under an extreme lateral shift a branch edge can physically overlap a neighbouring trunk B-edge, so the DP mis-assigns topology; the re-match cannot repair a wrong topology, and validation flags the resulting backward step rather than hiding it.

8.2 The realised-cost objective

Reported quality is the realised drift from the final consistent labels,

(11) Ctotal = ΣaE(a,φ(a))

with the headline metric the average drift Ctotal/(matched steps) in metres — comparable to graph-DTW's mean matched distance — supplemented by a per-A-edge breakdown and coverage percentage. Two quantities must not be reported as the total. The DP optimum Σsinks minv D[t][v] (dp_cost) is the DP's discrete, unconstrained optimum — every sink free to pick its cheapest sampled B-vertex — and is tree-only exact and not a bound on Ctotal (§7.1): continuous re-match can beat it, while joint-consistency-under-shift pushes far above it (shift-4 y_split: dp 61.7 vs realised 108.3; diamond: dp 96.9 vs realised 183.6). Likewise the junction identity D+B−E is a tree-only reading. Equation (9) is the definition; the DP quantities are diagnostics.

On a clean tree the DP optimum and the realised total coincide, which makes a worked check transparent. For a Y-split a0→a1, a1→{a2,a3} with every vertex drifting 0.2 m and outdeg(a1) = 2:

D[a0] = 0.2,    D[a1] = 0.4,    D[a2] = 0.2 + ½ · 0.4 = 0.4,    D[a3] = 0.4

so Σsinks {a2,a3} = 0.8 = 0.2 · 4, matching the four-vertex realised drift. A naive sum without the 1/outdeg split would double-count a0,a1 and give 1.2 — wrong. Ctotal is count-weighted (hence density-dependent through step_meters); a length-weighted, density-independent objective is future work.

8.3 The coverage weight α

The plain horizontal move re-pays the emission at every covered B-vertex (§4.2), so a 1:N coverage costs linearly in B's sampling density — an artefact of Δ, not of geometry. The weight α = horizontal_weight ≤ 1 discounts it. The correct fix discounts the emission, not the carried cost:

(12) D[a][v] = α E(a,v) + min( (H), (A) ),    α = { horizontal_weight if (H) wins (coverage extends); 1 if (A) wins (a new A-vertex's first match) }

Multiplying the carried cost instead would make the horizontal edge weight negative for α < 1, letting the DP "launder" cost by wandering through cheap B-vertices (measured to shift diamond average drift 0.55 → 0.74); that variant was rejected. Unrolling a coverage v0 → … → vk at drift δ, the first vertex is entered by A-advance at full δ and each of the remaining k = N−1 by a discounted horizontal step, so the coverage costs

(13) δ ( 1 + α k )

Thus α = 1 reproduces today's per-B-point charge bit-for-bit, while α → 0 charges a coverage essentially once, sampling-independently (e.g. at δ = 0.4, N = 30: 12.0 m at α = 1, 0.4 m at α = 0). Because α E ≥ 0 remains a non-negative per-step emission, D never decreases along a coverage — no laundering — and the routing decision min(H,A) on carried cost is unchanged; only the charged emission differs. Implementation-wise α = 1 leaves the Dijkstra untouched, while α ≠ 1 resolves (H) in B-topological order (_relax_alpha), with bounded iterative relaxation as a cyclic-B fallback (the map contracts for α < 1). The weight lives inside the decision cost D,B, so it shifts which φ is selected — toward more 1:N coverage — while the reported total and average drift remain the raw drift ΣE of that φ. It is orthogonal to the junction-label and nearest-vs-corresponding problems; default α = 1.0.

8.4 Output

match_dag_to_bgraph returns the junction-consistent label map φ, a per-vertex match record, terse per-A-edge routes, the realised total_cost (9), the dp_cost diagnostic, avg_drift, the source/sink sets, and optional debug tables. The downstream-ready face is routes_detail (_route_detail), one entry per A-edge carrying its route, average and maximum drift, point count, covered length, the boundary start/end positions, and per-edge partial-coverage fractions tfrom, tto ∈ [0,1] (fractional arc-length along each B-edge in its own travel direction, via shapely.project(normalized=True)) with cover_pct = (tto − tfrom) · 100. Only the first and last B-edge of a route can be partial; interior edges are full. These fractions are computed purely from the arc-length-re-matched φ and the B-geometries with no extra DP, so the emitted coverage is directed and jump-free by construction.

10 Discussion & Limitations

The evaluation supports a specific, bounded claim: on directed source DAGs whose undirected skeleton is a tree/polytree (|F| = 0), the shipped point-mode matcher produces junction-consistent, sequence-rule-clean matchings and is robust to large rigid perturbation; and the two conditioning solvers form an exact, mutually-validated oracle for the labelling problem on any DAG. The limitations below are equally important and are stated so the reader can calibrate what the method does and does not yet deliver.

10.1 The diamond needs the direction term, not more loop machinery

The one residual failure in the perturbation sweep is the reconvergent diamond under extreme rigid shift (≥ 8 m in the synthetic case), where the two A-branches collapse onto the single nearer B-edge. It would be tempting to read this as a failure of forward–backward message passing on an undirected cycle — and indeed message passing is only exact on trees (Section 7). But the honest finding, confirmed by the exact conditioning solver, is that the collapse is the true minimum-drift optimum in point mode: at that shift, both branches riding the nearer B-edge genuinely costs less total drift than the correct correspondence, and the correct split never appears at any cut label. The exact solvers reproduce the collapse, so conditioning reduces but does not remove this error.

The diagnosis is therefore that this is a property of the objective, not of the inference. A middle-to-middle point distance is blind to a segment rotating about its own centre and, under a large lateral offset, to which of two nearby parallel edges is the corresponding one rather than the nearest one. The fix is the direction term — segment+bearing emission (Section 11) — which pins heading and separates parallel edges by orientation. No amount of additional loop-conditioning machinery in point mode can recover the correspondence, because the correspondence is not the point-mode minimum.

A related caveat is that the DP's internal optimum must not be reported as the realized cost. The realized total Ctotal = Σa E(a,φ(a)) is always correct, but the conserved-flow reading Σsinks minv D[t][v] (dp_cost) and the joint-junction score D[j][v] + B[j][v] − E(j,v) are exact only on a tree. On a reconvergent diamond the two tables' splits do not cancel when combined at one junction; measured on a perturbed diamond, D+B−E at the split reads 112.8 while Σsinks min D reads 96.9 (they agree exactly on every tree). dp_cost is thus a DP diagnostic, not a bound on the realized cost in either direction, and only total_cost should be reported as a quality figure.

10.2 Adversarial cross-validation found and fixed two real bugs

Because the two conditioning solvers must return equal-cost labellings, any disagreement localizes a defect — the cross-validation is itself the test harness. This workflow surfaced and fixed two genuine correctness bugs, both triggered by anti-parallel B geometry (a B-edge digitized against A's flow, which is entirely normal in real conflation, e.g. NVDB is_reverse twins):

  1. Infeasibility mis-scored as cheap-and-valid. _forest_solve had re-summed drift over an argmin=0 fallback of an all-∞ belief, so an infeasible pinned boundary (an A-arc forced to run backward in B) was scored as a cheap valid labelling, undercutting the true optimum. The fix returns the BP value itself as the cost (+∞ when infeasible), propagating infeasibility instead of laundering it.
  2. Inter-F (internal) backward arcs invisible. The forest solver only folded pinned-to-free constraints, so arcs internal to the pinned set that ran backward in B were never checked. The fix (_pins_feasible) rejects any pinning whose internal arcs run backward.

An earlier symptom of the same class: on double_diamond the two methods first disagreed (29.09 vs 29.33) when the forest base was the heuristic reachability-guarded backtrack; replacing that base with exact min-sum belief propagation made both agree at 28.930 — below either heuristic value. The lesson generalizes: conditioning buys exactness only on top of an exact forest solver, and the labels — not the loop structure — are the hard part.

10.3 Candidate-restriction divergence on adversarial multi-loop inputs

With a full candidate set (cand_k = NB) the two solvers agree on any DAG. Under the default K-nearest restriction (CAND_K = 12) they agree on realistic conflation inputs and on all five scenarios in the sweep, but they can diverge — while both still returning valid labellings — on adversarial anti-parallel, multi-loop geometry, where the feasible optimum's labels sit beyond the nearest few B-vertices. This is a candidate-restriction limit that raising cand_k closes, documented rather than a solver defect.

10.4 Scope and shipped-vs-reference status

Three scope caveats bound the claims:

  • The conditioning solvers are reference/validation only. They are deliberately not wired into match_dag_to_bgraph; the shipped matcher keeps the reachability-guarded forward–backward backtrack. Consequently shipped junction consistency is region-consistent (within one sample step), not vertex-exact — vertex-exact resolution requires the conditioning solvers, which remain a validation oracle in v1.
  • Point-mode, synthetic-only numbers. Every figure in Section 9 is point-mode DAG-DTW on the five hand-built DAGs at a constant offset δ = 0.4 m in a plain-metre CRS. They validate the correctness and exactness of the DP and the conditioning, not accuracy on real OSM↔NVDB data, and not the segment+bearing regime that the design intends for production.
  • Count-weighted objective. Ctotal sums per-vertex emissions and is therefore density-dependent through step_meters; a length-weighted, density-independent objective is future work.

11 Conclusion & Future Work

We have presented a projection-based, directed-graph generalization of dynamic time warping for road-network conflation. Graph-DTW aligns a single directed A-edge to a local directed target graph GB that may contain cycles, replacing DTW's scalar predecessor with a Dijkstra over GB's arcs on the horizontal move; DAG-DTW lifts the source from a path to a connected acyclic subgraph GA, solving a whole junction neighbourhood in one topological sweep so that A-edges meeting at a junction agree on where that junction lands in B. The 1/outdeg split factor gives a conserved cost-flow that makes each vertex's emission contribute exactly once across all sinks for any DAG shape, and forward–backward resolution with a reachability-guarded backtrack pins junctions jointly. On tree/polytree sources this regime is exact and robust; two independent exact conditioning solvers — recursive minimum-vertex-cut and one-shot minimum-feedback-vertex-set over a shared min-sum belief-propagation forest base — provide a mutually-validated oracle (225/225 agreement, 238/238 validator checks, brute-force-anchored) that both certifies the tree regime and characterizes the reconvergent one.

The evaluation is deliberately honest about the boundary of the shipped point-mode matcher: the reconvergent diamond under extreme lateral offset collapses onto the nearer B-edge, and the exact solver confirms this is the genuine point-mode minimum-drift optimum. The correspondence problem there is a property of the objective, not the inference. Two lines of future work follow directly.

Segment+bearing DAG-DTW ("v2"). The single most important extension is to carry the middle-to-middle segment emission with the bearing term,

(14) E(i,e) = &lVert;mid(ai,ai+1) − mid(u,v)&rVert; + λ dcirc(θ(si), θ(e)),

already shipped and tested in graph-DTW, into the DAG DP. Because the bearing term pins heading and separates geometrically-close parallel edges by orientation, it is the principled fix for the nearest-vs-corresponding collapse — not more loop machinery. The recommended working regime (λ ∈ [1, 5]) is established for graph-DTW; porting it to the joint junction solve is the natural next milestone.

Exact reconvergence in the shipped matcher. The conditioning solvers exist and are validated but live alongside the matcher as a reference oracle. Wiring the recursive minimum-vertex-cut solver into match_dag_to_bgraph — invoked only on the (typically tiny, |F|-small) reconvergent components a real network presents — would upgrade shipped junction consistency from region-consistent to vertex-exact on genuine loops at linear cost in the number of loops, while leaving the exact tree regime untouched.

Further work includes a length-weighted, sampling-density-independent objective; a symmetric B → A reconciliation pass; and a cyclic-source fallback to per-edge graph-DTW for local components whose source side is not acyclic (the matcher presently raises NotADAG). Validation on real OSM↔NVDB corridors — the setting the method was designed for — is the essential empirical next step beyond the synthetic correctness suite reported here.

Appendix A — DP recurrences

This appendix states the two cost tables of DAG-DTW in full, derives the split factor and the conserved-flow identity, and works the Y-split example by hand. Throughout, GA is the source local DAG (topologically ordered A-vertices) and GB the target local directed graph (B-vertices, possibly cyclic). E(a,v) is the emission of Appendix E; in point mode E(a,v) = ‖a−v‖. Apred(a) and Bpred(v) denote graph-predecessors in GA and GB, and outdeg(a) the out-degree of a in GA.

A.1 The forward table D

D[a][v] is the minimum cost to align the source graph down to a — with every A-edge topologically above a covered — ending with a labelled at B-vertex v. The defining recurrence has two operators, a horizontal move (H) in which B advances while a stays fixed, and an advance move (A) in which the sweep moves to a from its A-predecessors:

(15) D[a][v] = E(a,v) + min( minv′∈Bpred(v) D[a][v′] (H) , Σa′∈Apred(a) 1outdeg(a′) minv′∈Bpred(v)∪{v} D[a′][v′] (A) )

The (A) branch encodes two structural facts:

  1. Sum over Apred(a) (coverage, not routing). At a merge, the costs of all incoming A-edges are added. D[a][v] is not "the cheapest path through GA to a"; it is "the cheapest way to align everything above a, with a pinned to v." A min here would silently discard an incoming branch. The rule is therefore sum over branches, min over B-positions.
  2. The three DTW moves are the chain special case. When a has a single predecessor a′ with outdeg(a′) = 1, the (A) sum is a single term minv′∈Bpred(v)∪{v} D[a′][v′], and expanding the outer min gives exactly
(16) D[a][v] = E(a,v) + min( D[a][v′] horizontal , D[a′][v] vertical , D[a′][v′] diagonal ),   v′∈Bpred(v)

i.e. the graph-DTW recurrence (vertical = A advances on the same B-vertex, horizontal = B advances on the same A-vertex, diagonal = both), with the scalar DTW predecessor j−1 replaced by "any B-predecessor of v" (Sakoe & Chiba, 1978; Berndt & Clifford, 1994). DAG-DTW is thus a strict superset of graph-DTW: a single-path GA is graph-DTW.

Boundary conditions arise with no special-casing.

  • Source A-vertex (Apred(a) = ∅): the (A) sum is empty = 0, so D[a][v] = E(a,v) — free entry at every source B-vertex.
  • Sink A-vertex: no outgoing edge consumes D[a][·]; the sweep leaves it free — free exit.

The horizontal move is a Dijkstra. Operator (H) self-references D[a][·] within the same A-vertex, and GB may contain directed cycles (roundabouts, loops), so there is no topological sweep of B. For fixed a it is a single-source-free non-negative shortest-path problem, solved exactly by Dijkstra (Dijkstra, 1959) over GB's arcs, re-paying the emission on each relaxation (so one A-vertex may ride a run of B-vertices when B is denser than A). Concretely (_dp_table, dag_dtw.py:184), one first seeds every B-vertex with the emission plus the split-scaled predecessor sum,

(17) seed[v] = E(a,v) + Σa′∈Apred(a) D[a′][v]outdeg(a′)

and then relaxes D[a][w] ← min( D[a][w], D[a][u] + E(a,w) ) over arcs u→w of GB. The diagonal term v′∈Bpred(v) is subsumed as the first relaxation of the Dijkstra. Because GA is acyclic, every D[a′][·] with a′∈Apred(a) is already finalised when the sweep reaches a (Kahn, 1962), so no outer iteration-to-convergence is ever needed.

A.2 The split factor and the conserved-flow identity

The factor 1/outdeg(a′) is what makes the objective well-defined on an arbitrary DAG. Read it as a cost-flow conservation law: each A-vertex divides its accumulated cost equally among its outgoing A-edges, so that — summed across all sinks — every vertex's emission E is counted exactly once, for any DAG shape (diamonds included). Chain and merge vertices have outdeg = 1 (factor 1, no division); only split vertices divide.

Formally, define the conserved-flow reading

(18) dp_cost = Σsinks t minv D[t][v]

Identity (tree case). If GA is a tree/polytree, then dp_cost = Σa E(a,φ(a)) = Ctotal, the realized cost of the labelling φ recovered by backtracking.

Argument. On a tree, orient each A-vertex a toward the unique sink(s) it feeds. Along the (A) branch, a's emission E(a,φ(a)) is multiplied on the way to each descendant sink by the product of 1/outdeg over the splits on that path. The number of sink-paths leaving a equals ∏ outdeg over exactly those same splits, and each path carries an equal share; the shares therefore sum to 1. Summing minv D[t][v] over the sinks re-assembles each emission with total weight 1, so the sum telescopes to Σa E(a,φ(a)). ∎

Caveat (reconvergent case). The identity is tree-only. On a reconvergent diamond (Appendix C), D conserves flow on its own and the backward table B conserves flow on its own, but combining them at one junction is weighted by both splits, which do not cancel; dp_cost is then a DP diagnostic, not a bound on Ctotal in either direction. The reported quality metric is always the realized

(19) Ctotal = Σa E(a,φ(a))

read off the final consistent φ (§A.4, Appendix B), never dp_cost.

A.3 The backward table B

B[a][v] is the mirror of D: the minimum cost to align a→ sinks with a pinned at v. It is produced by the same _dp_table after reversing both graphs — GA's sinks become sources (topological order reversed) and every GB arc is flipped — and replacing the split factor by the symmetric 1/indeg, so conserved flow holds in the reverse direction too:

(20) B[a][v] = E(a,v) + min( minv′∈Bsucc(v) B[a][v′] , Σa′∈Asucc(a) 1indeg(a′) minv′∈Bsucc(v)∪{v} B[a′][v′] )

where Bsucc / Asucc are successors and indeg the in-degree in GA. D and B together drive the junction resolution of Appendix B.

A.4 Worked Y-split example

Take the clean Y-split: A-vertices a0→a1, then a1→a2 and a1→a3, so a0 is the source, a1 the split (outdeg(a1) = 2), a2,a3 the sinks. Suppose every A-vertex drifts a constant δ = 0.2 onto its intended B-vertex, and (for a clean hand-check) that the horizontal and diagonal moves never beat the vertical advance, so each D picks up one E = 0.2 plus its split-scaled predecessor.

(21) D[a0] = 0.2   (source: free entry, E only)
D[a1] = 0.2 + 11·D[a0] = 0.2 + 0.2 = 0.4   (outdeg(a0) = 1)
D[a2] = 0.2 + 12·D[a1] = 0.2 + ½(0.4) = 0.4   (split share 1/outdeg(a1) = ½)
D[a3] = 0.2 + 12·D[a1] = 0.4

The conserved-flow reading over the two sinks is

(22) dp_cost = minv D[a2][v] + minv D[a3][v] = 0.4 + 0.4 = 0.8 = 0.2·4

which is exactly δ times the four A-vertices — each emission counted once. A naive sum without the split factor would give D[a2] = D[a3] = 0.2 + 0.4 = 0.6 and dp_cost = 1.2, double-counting a0 and a1. Because the Y-split is a tree, the identity of §A.2 holds and dp_cost = Ctotal = 0.8; the two coincide here precisely because a clean tree's DP optimum is already junction-consistent.

Appendix B — Junction resolution & exactness

The DP of Appendix A produces two cost tables, not a labelling. This appendix explains why the labelling cannot be read off per-junction, gives the forward–backward joint estimator and the reachability-guarded backtrack that turn D,B into a consistent φ, and proves exactness on trees.

B.1 Why per-junction argmin is inconsistent

Consider two routes s1→t1 and s2→t2 that pass through a common junction j∈GA. Optimised independently, route 1 prefers j at one B-vertex and route 2 at a different one — but j carries only one label φ(j). A greedy per-sink backtrack that traces each route back from its own cheapest sink will have the two traces collide at j with incompatible demands; forcing a single label satisfies one route and forces the other to take a backward step — a non-forward B-transition on a GA arc, i.e. a route that must run against B's digitisation to reach its next fixed label. Crucially this happens even when every A-edge is mapped to the correct B-edge: the failure is in the shared position of the junction, not the edge assignment. One therefore cannot assemble the whole matching from each-sink's-own-best pieces.

B.2 The forward–backward joint estimator

The fix is to score each junction once, for everyone passing through it, using both tables. For a junction A-vertex j,

(23) φ(j) = argminv ( D[j][v] + B[j][v] − E(j,v) )

D[j][v] is the cheapest alignment of sources → j with j at v; B[j][v] the cheapest alignment of j→ sinks with j at v; their sum is the best whole-DAG cost among all matchings that keep j at v. The −E(j,v) removes the double count, since j's own emission sits in both tables (dag_dtw.py:522). This is a min-sum message combination (Pearl, 1988): D and B are the incoming forward/backward messages at j, and the estimator marginalises everything else exactly on a tree.

B.3 The reachability-guarded backtrack (shipped procedure)

The joint score alone still permits ties and near-ties that are mutually inconsistent across junctions. The shipped resolver (dag_dtw.py:520–535) therefore sweeps A-vertices in reverse-topological order and, at each non-sink a, scans v in increasing joint score D[a][v] + B[a][v] − E(a,v) and commits the cheapest v that still forward-reaches every already-fixed successor's label — a BFS reachability test in GB from v to each φ(a′), a′∈Asucc(a). Sinks take the unconstrained argmin (free exit). The reachability constraint is the cheap enforcement of joint consistency: it discards exactly the inconsistent tie-breaks that a rigid per-vertex shift would create. Dropping the guard (pure per-vertex argmin(D+B−E)) measurably regresses merge and y_split under perturbation.

A final arc-length re-match (Appendix F of the main text; _arclength_rematch, dag_dtw.py:278) then fixes the continuous position of each A-vertex on its route's B-polyline, so the DP decides topology and the re-match decides geometry jump-free.

B.4 Tree-exactness

Claim. If GA is a tree/polytree (no two directed paths from a common ancestor re-meet), the forward–backward estimator with the reachability-guarded backtrack returns the globally minimum-Ctotal junction-consistent labelling.

Argument. On a tree, removing any junction j splits GA into independent components — the "above j" subtree and each "below j" subtree, which share no other A-vertex. The forward message D[j][·] exactly summarises the optimal cost of the above part as a function of φ(j), and the backward messages summarise each below part; because the components are otherwise disjoint, their costs are additive and D[j][v] + B[j][v] − E(j,v) is the exact minimum whole-tree cost conditioned on φ(j) = v (standard min-sum exactness on a tree; Pearl, 1988). Sweeping in reverse-topological order and fixing each j to the cheapest reachability-feasible v commits labels only after all its descendants are fixed, so the reachability test is against already-optimal targets and never forces a suboptimal parent. Hence every junction receives its globally optimal consistent label, and no backward step is created. ∎

Empirically (§G, 512-config perturbation sweep) the backward step is eliminated on chain, merge, and y_split; the only structure that still fails is the reconvergent diamond, which is not a tree and is treated in Appendix C. require_tree=True (dag_dtw.py:460, via the cyclomatic number E−V+C = 0) asserts this exact regime for callers who know their source neighbourhood is a polytree; interior degree-2 sampling vertices never add a cycle, so a diamond is detected regardless of sampling density.

Appendix C — Reconvergence conditioning

A tree is the exact regime (Appendix B). This appendix treats the one structure that escapes it — a reconvergent diamond — and the two exact reference solvers (dag_conditioning.py) that resolve it. These solvers are [REF]: validated, and deliberately not wired into the shipped matcher, for the honest reason established at the end.

C.1 The structural problem

An undirected cycle in a DAG occurs exactly where two directed paths from a common ancestor re-meet: a diamond with split j1→{up,down}→ merge j2. On such a cycle the two branches share both endpoints. Forward–backward lets each side choose its own label where the sides re-meet — B[j1][v] sums the branches as if each could independently pick j2 — so the two disagree and a backward step appears. The reachability guard of Appendix B is insufficient because the loop constrains j1 and j2 simultaneously in both directions; min-sum message passing is exact only when the graph has no undirected cycle (Pearl, 1988).

Two facts follow that the paper must state to avoid over-claiming (verified on a perturbed diamond): D+B−E at the split reads 112.8 while Σsinks min D reads 96.9 — they agree exactly on every tree but not here — and neither equals the realized Ctotal (diamond dp_cost = 96.9 vs realized 183.6 under a joint-consistent shift). So dp_cost is a DP diagnostic, not a bound.

C.2 The exact min-sum belief-propagation forest solver

Both conditioning solvers rest on one exact base, _forest_solve (dag_conditioning.py:140): min-sum (max-product) belief propagation on a polytree (Pearl, 1988). The model is a pairwise MRF over A-vertex labels:

  • Unary potential ψa(v) = E(a,v) plus any folded constraints from pinned-boundary reachability (an A-vertex adjacent to a pinned junction inherits a hard +∞ where it cannot forward-reach that pin).
  • Pairwise potential on each GA arc a→a′: ψa,a′(v,v′) = 0 if v′∈ReachGB(v) (the directed reachability constraint φ(head)∈Reach(φ(tail))) and +∞ otherwise.

On any tree this returns the exact minimum, and — the correctness-critical detail — it returns the BP value itself (+∞ for an infeasible pinning), never a drift re-summed off an argmin fallback (see §C.5). Conditioning is exact only because this base is exact; the reachability-guarded backtrack of Appendix B is a heuristic and cannot serve as the base (§C.5).

C.3 Solver 1 — recursive minimum-vertex-cut

_solve_recursive: if the free part of GA is already a forest, run BP and return. Otherwise pick a small vertex cut S (in practice the min-feedback-vertex-set vertices, usually a single junction), enumerate its K-nearest B-candidates, pin S to each combination, and recurse into the now-independent components GA−S. Complexity (#cand)w where w is the largest single cut (usually 1) — linear in the number of loops: a chain of k diamonds costs ∼ k·#cand.

C.4 Solver 2 — one-shot minimum feedback-vertex-set

_solve_fvs (with min_feedback_vertex_set): compute a minimum FVS F — the smallest vertex set whose removal leaves a forest — enumerate all joint label combinations of F at once, and solve the forest GA−F with BP for each. Complexity (#cand)|F| — exponential in the number of loops. FVS describes when the problem is trivial (F = ∅ ⇔ tree ⇔ shipped solver already exact) but is a poor way to label at scale; it exists as the independent cross-check. Finding a minimum cut / FVS is NP-hard in general (Karp, 1972), but local junction graphs are tiny and a greedy separator suffices.

C.5 Why the two must agree, and the bugs the agreement caught

Both solvers minimise the same realized cost Σa E(a,φ(a)) with the same exact forest base, differing only in how they cut loops. Therefore, with a full candidate set, on any DAG they must return equal-cost labellings. This mutual invariant is itself the test harness: any disagreement localises a bug. Validation (scripts/dag_conditioning_validate.py, re-run 2026-07-07) passes 238/238 checks — e.g. diamond recursive = fvs = 24.581; double_diamond (|F| = 2, the case that actually separates the two methods) = 28.930 for both; BP = independent brute-force optimum on tiny cases; K-nearest = all-44-candidates on the diamond; 225/225 configs agree across the 5×3×3 perturbation grid.

The invariant caught two genuine correctness bugs (commit d289ffe), both triggered by anti-parallel B geometry (a B-edge digitised against A's flow — normal in real conflation):

  1. Infeasibility mis-scored as cheap-and-valid. _forest_solve had re-summed drift over an argmin=0 fallback of an all-∞ belief, so an infeasible pinned boundary (an A-arc forced backward in B) was scored as a cheap valid labelling, undercutting the true optimum. Fix: return the BP value itself as the cost, propagating +∞ instead of laundering it.
  2. Inter-F backward arcs invisible. The forest solver folds only pinned-to-free constraints, so arcs internal to the pinned set running backward were never checked. Fix: _pins_feasible rejects any pinning whose internal arcs run backward in B.

An earlier symptom of the same class: double_diamond first disagreed (29.09 vs 29.33) when the forest base was the heuristic Appendix-B backtrack; swapping in exact min-sum BP made both agree at 28.93 — below either heuristic value. The lesson the paper should carry: the labels are the hard part; conditioning buys exactness only on top of an exact forest solver.

C.6 The honest limit — the diamond needs the direction term, not a cut

The decisive finding is that the diamond's failure is not a loop-conditioning failure. Pin the split j1 to the exact B-split and solve the branches in point mode: both A-branches still collapse onto the nearer B-edge, because that genuinely costs less — the correct split never appears at any cut label. The exact conditioning solvers only confirm the collapse is the true minimum-drift optimum in point mode. Conditioning therefore reduces but does not remove the nearest-vs-corresponding error; the real fix is the direction term (segment / bearing emission, Appendix E), which is why the shipped point-mode matcher keeps the Appendix-B backtrack and holds these solvers alongside as reference and validation only.

Appendix D — Coverage weight α

When B is sampled more densely than A, one A-vertex must cover a run of B-vertices via repeated horizontal (H) moves. The plain (H) move re-pays the emission E(a,·) at every covered B-vertex, so a 1:N match costs linearly in B's sampling density — an arbitrary artefact of Δ. The horizontal_weight α ≤ 1 (dag_dtw.py, default 1.0) discounts it. [IMPL]

D.1 Discount the emission, not the carried cost

The subtle and important choice is what α multiplies. Multiplying the carried cost would make the horizontal edge weight negative for α < 1, and the DP would then "launder" cost by wandering through cheap B-vertices (measured to shift diamond avg-drift 0.55 → 0.74; rejected). Instead α scales only the emission charged on a horizontal step:

(24) D[a][v] = α E(a,v) + min( (H), (A) ),    α = { horizontal_weight if (H) wins (reached by extending coverage);   1 if (A) wins (a new A-vertex's first match) }

The first match of any A-vertex is entered by an A-advance and pays the full emission; only the continuation of a coverage run is discounted.

D.2 Coverage-cost derivation

Unroll a single coverage v0→v1→…→vk at a constant drift δ. Vertex v0 is entered by A-advance (full δ); each of the remaining k vertices is entered by a horizontal move (αδ each). The total coverage cost is

(25) δ( 1 + α k ),    k = N−1 = extra covered B-vertices

α = 1 recovers today's per-B-point charge δN (bit-for-bit unchanged); α → 0 charges the coverage essentially once (δ, sampling-independent). Verified directly against _relax_alpha at δ = 0.4:

D.3 No-laundering argument

Because αE ≥ 0 is a non-negative local emission added on every step, the carried cost D is non-decreasing along a coverage run: D[vi] ≥ D[vi−1] for all i (checked at α = 0.3, test_horizontal_weight_coverage_cost_and_monotone). The routing decision min( (H), (A) ) operates on the carried cost, which α never touches; only the emission charged differs. Hence the DP can never lower its running total by wandering — the pathology that motivated rejecting the carried-cost variant is structurally impossible here.

D.4 B-topological-order relaxation

Implementation detail: at α = 1 the Dijkstra is untouched (exact no-op — bit-for-bit identical φ and total_cost on every scenario, test_horizontal_weight_one_is_unchanged). At α ≠ 1 the (H) term is resolved in B-topological order (_relax_alpha, dag_dtw.py:252), which lets the discounted emission propagate along a coverage run in one pass; on a cyclic B component a bounded iterative relaxation is the fallback, and it converges because α < 1 makes the horizontal operator a contraction. α lives inside the decision cost (D,B), so it shifts which φ is chosen (toward more 1:N coverage — confirmed on the shifted diamond, test_horizontal_weight_extends_coverage), but the reported total_cost / avg_drift remain the raw Σ drift of that φ. It is orthogonal to the junction-label and nearest-vs-corresponding problems.

Appendix E — Emission modes

The emission E(a,v) is the only place the geometry enters the DP, and is identical in graph-DTW and DAG-DTW. Two modes ship.

E.1 Point mode (default) [IMPL]

(26) E(a,v) = ‖a−v‖   (Euclidean, metres)

A B-vertex is scored purely on proximity. This is the mode all synthetic numbers in Appendix G measure. Its limitation is exactly the nearest-vs-corresponding collapse of Appendix C: distance alone cannot distinguish two parallel B-edges, so under a large lateral offset the DP prefers the nearer one even when it is the wrong correspondent.

E.2 Segment mode (middle-to-middle + bearing) [IMPL]

Segment mode is a true (A-segment, B-arc)-state DP (_segment_dp_pairs, graph_dtw.py:301). Its state is (i,e) = "A-segment i rides B-arc e = u→v", and its per-state cost is the middle-to-middle distance plus an optional bearing term:

(27) E(i,e) = ‖ mid(ai,ai+1) − mid(u,v) ‖ + λ·dcirc( θ(si), θ(e) )

where mid(·,·) is a segment midpoint, θ a heading, λ = bearing_weight, and dcirc ∈ [0°,180°] the circular heading difference (so a segment and its reverse are 180° apart). The three DP moves become A-advance on the same arc (N:1), arc-advance within a row (Dijkstra, 1:N), or both; the arc-state path is emitted as vertex pairs so all downstream grouping and metrics are shared with point mode.

Bearing convention. θ(e) is the direction of travel along the forward B-arc u→v; θ(si) the direction of the A-segment. Because the local graphs contain forward arcs only and a bidirectional road is two opposing directed edges, a heading difference near 180° signals a wrong-direction (reverse-twin) match and is penalised out.

Why the bearing term, not distance alone. A middle-to-middle distance is blind to a segment rotating about its own middle — two crossing segments sharing a midpoint have zero distance cost. The bearing term is what pins heading, so the recommended working configuration is emission="segment" with λ ∈ [1,5].

E.3 The diamond-fix cost argument

This is the emission-level resolution of the Appendix-C limit. On the reconvergent diamond under lateral shift, point mode collapses both A-branches onto the nearer B-edge because the distance saved by collapsing genuinely exceeds the distance lost. But the correct correspondent branch and the nearer wrong branch differ in heading: collapsing forces the A-segment onto a B-arc whose bearing disagrees, incurring λ·dcirc on every covered segment. For λ large enough that the accumulated bearing penalty exceeds the distance saved, the true split re-emerges as the minimum. This is why the honest conclusion of Appendix C is "the diamond needs the direction term, not a cut": the direction term changes the objective so that the correct match is genuinely cheapest, whereas conditioning only searches the point-mode objective more exactly. Wiring segment+bearing into DAG-DTW ("v2") is the flagged [DESIGN] future work.

Historical accuracy. docs/weighted_emission.md §2–§10 develop two earlier variants (endpoint-average distance; bearing charged only on diagonal moves); both were superseded by §11's middle-to-middle form, which is what ships. emission="midpoint" is a deprecated alias for "segment". Only the middle-to-middle form should be cited.

Appendix F — Complexity

Let N be the number of densified A-vertices in the local problem, and VB,EB the vertices and arcs of the local target graph GB. All local graphs are small (a junction neighbourhood), so the polylogarithmic factors are on tiny inputs.

Emission. E(a,v) is O(1) (a distance, plus O(1) bearing in segment mode). The projection-based builder build_local_digraph (graph_dtw.py:202) constructs both vertex pools in O( (VA+VB)2 ) worst case (each side's nodes projected onto the other side's edges), typically far less.

Graph-DTW (one A-edge). The DP has N rows; each row's horizontal move is a Dijkstra over GB, O(EB log VB), and vertical/diagonal are O(VB). Total per A-edge:

(28) O( N·EB log VB )

Segment mode multiplies VB by the arc-state factor but stays the same asymptotic form over GB's arcs.

DAG-DTW (one source neighbourhood). A single topological sweep of GA (Kahn, 1962) visits each A-vertex once; per vertex it seeds (O(VB) over predecessors) and runs one within-a Dijkstra. With N A-vertices,

(29) O( N·EB log VB ) forward + the same for the backward table B = O( N·EB log VB )

Because GA is acyclic, no iteration-to-convergence is needed; the α ≠ 1 relaxation adds at most a bounded constant number of passes on a cyclic B component (contraction, Appendix D). The reachability-guarded backtrack (Appendix B) is a reverse-topological sweep with a BFS reachability test per committed vertex, O( N·(VB+EB) ). Arc-length re-match is O(N). So the shipped matcher is linear in N up to the small EB log VB target factor.

Conditioning solvers [REF]. Let w be the largest single vertex cut and |F| the minimum-FVS size, and c = #cand the candidate count per pinned vertex (K-nearest, default 12).

  • Recursive min-vertex-cut: O(cw) forest solves — linear in the number of loops (each loop cut independently, usually w = 1); a chain of k diamonds is ∼ k·c.
  • One-shot min-FVS: O(c|F|) forest solves — exponential in the number of loops.

Each forest solve is one exact min-sum BP pass over the polytree, O(N·c2) in the worst pairwise case on tiny local graphs. Computing a minimum cut / FVS is NP-hard in general (Karp, 1972) but trivial at junction scale (a greedy separator suffices).

G.3 Sequence rules

check_sequence_rules (dag_dtw.py:89) enforces three invariants on the matched result, reported as arc_viol / route_viol / jump_viol:

  1. Monotone forward B-walk — every GA arc a→a′ maps to a forward-reachable B-step φ(a)→φ(a′) (no backward/disconnected jump).
  2. Connected route — consecutive B-edges of each route are graph-connected in GB.
  3. No teleport — the B-advance tracks the A-advance (B-step − A-step < 3 m; no junction jump).

At the clean pose all counts are zero on all five scenarios, and junction region-consistency holds: coincident A-vertices at a junction land within one sample step of a common B-location (measured spread 0.00 m — 4/4 junctions on diamond, 8/8 on double_diamond). Region-consistency is the v1 point-mode guarantee; vertex-exact junction resolution is what the conditioning solvers add.

  • The diamond needs the direction term. Under extreme shift (≥ 8 m here) point mode collapses; conditioning confirms this optimum rather than removing it. The fix is segment/bearing emission (Appendix E), design/future work.
