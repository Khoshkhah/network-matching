Directed-Network Conflation by Dynamic Time Warping on Graphs: Route-Consistent Map-to-Map Matching under Divergent Segmentation

Kaveh · Sonocount Research
network-matching library · working paper
Abstract

Conflation — the problem of deciding which features of one geographic map correspond to which features of another — is a prerequisite for fusing attributes across independently maintained road datasets (e.g. transferring traffic counts from a national road register onto an OpenStreetMap network). We address the directed, route-valued form of the problem: given two directed road networks A and B that model the same physical streets but are segmented differently, assign to each directed edge of A a connected, direction-respecting route of B-edges that minimises geometric shape drift while remaining junction-consistent — edges of A that meet at a junction must agree on where that junction lands in B. Classical curve-alignment and GPS-trajectory map-matching methods do not fit: the former align two open curves, not a curve to a directed graph with branches and cycles, and the latter match a noisy point sequence to a network but assume a single moving agent rather than a whole branching sub-map that must be labelled coherently. We develop a family of alignment algorithms built on Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) (Sakoe & Chiba, 1978; Berndt & Clifford, 1994). Graph-DTW warps a single directed A-edge against a local directed target graph GB that may contain cycles, replacing DTW's scalar predecessor with a within-row Dijkstra (Dijkstra, 1959) over GB's arcs; it supports a point emission and a segment emission with a bearing term that pins heading. DAG-DTW generalises the source from a single path to a whole acyclic branching neighbourhood GA: one forward table over radius-gated cells (source-vertex × candidate-target-vertex pairs), filled in longest-path layer order, with a split-factor recurrence that conserves cost-flow across branches and a forbid-and-rebuild coupling that enforces split consistency during the build. The extraction is a cell-level join: per-vertex tables built backward from the sinks out of the emissions alone — the forward table serving only as pruning — returning the cheapest root row that passes the four warping rules (V1)–(V4). It is exact over the full matching space, reconvergences (diamonds) included, verified 195/195 against full-space brute force. A vertex-level junction join, exact over its stored-history family, cross-validates it: on a structured 384-case envelope the cell join returns 384/384 valid matchings (vertex join 379/384), with the invariant C(cell) ≤ C(vertex join) pinned in the test suite. We formalise the problem, the objective, and the notion of junction consistency used throughout the paper.

1 Introduction

Maintaining more than one digital model of the same road system is the normal state of affairs, not the exception. A national road-attribute register, a crowd-sourced street map, a commercial navigation base, and a municipality's asset inventory each describe the same physical streets, but they are digitised independently: they cut a road into edges at different places, place junction nodes at slightly different coordinates, and disagree on geometry by a metre or more. To combine their attributes — to carry a per-minute traffic flow measured on the register's segmentation onto the crowd-sourced network's segmentation, say — one must first solve conflation: establish which feature of one map corresponds to which feature of the other (Newson & Krumm, 2009, frame the closely related applied setting).

For road networks the correspondence is neither one-to-one nor symmetric between features. Because the two maps are segmented differently, a single edge of the source map A typically spans several consecutive edges of the target map B, and conversely a long B-edge may absorb several short A-edges. The correct output is therefore not a node-to-node or edge-to-edge assignment but a route assignment: each directed A-edge is mapped to a connected, directed path of B-edges that it traverses. Three properties make this hard.

The networks are directed. A road carries a legal and geometric direction; a two-way street is modelled as two opposing directed edges (in the Swedish NVDB register, for example, as is_reverse twins). A correct matcher must never let a route run backward along a B-edge, and must never let a source path that happens to run against the flow of a nearby B-edge "borrow" that edge instead of its reverse twin. Undirected or symmetric shape-matching silently permits both.

The segmentation is divergent. Neither map's cut points are canonical. The alignment must be free to let one A-vertex cover a run of several B-vertices (when B is sampled more densely) and to let several A-vertices share one stretch of B — exactly the many-to-one warping that DTW was designed for, but here the "time axis" on the target side is a graph, with branches and cycles (roundabouts, divided-carriageway loops), not a line.

Junctions must stay consistent. Where several A-edges meet at a junction, they share a single physical point; the matching must place that shared point at a single location in B. If each A-edge is matched to its own individually-cheapest B-route, the routes generically disagree about where the junction lands — they collide at the junction and one of them is forced into a backward or disconnected step. Junction consistency cannot, in general, be assembled from per-edge-optimal pieces; it is a joint constraint over all edges incident to the junction.

Why existing methods do not fit. Two mature bodies of work are adjacent but miss the target.

Curve-to-curve alignment. DTW (Sakoe & Chiba, 1978; Berndt & Clifford, 1994) and the Fréchet/edit-distance family align two open, linearly-ordered curves. A road edge is such a curve, but a road neighbourhood is a directed graph: it branches, it merges, and it can contain directed cycles. There is no single monotone warping axis to align against, and no notion of a junction shared by several curves. Applying curve alignment edge-by-edge throws away precisely the junction-consistency coupling that makes conflation hard.

GPS-trajectory map-matching. HMM-style map-matching (Newson & Krumm, 2009) snaps a noisy, time-ordered sequence of points from a single moving vehicle onto a road network. It shares our directed-graph target and our shortest-path transitions, but its input is one thread, not a branching sub-map, and it has no obligation to keep several threads consistent where they cross. It answers "which road was this one trajectory on?", not "which route of B does each edge of a whole branching piece of A correspond to, such that they all agree at the junctions?".

Our approach keeps DTW's warping intuition but replaces both of its linear axes with graphs. On the target side, the horizontal (target-advances) move becomes a Dijkstra shortest-path relaxation over GB's arcs, so a single A-vertex may ride a run of B-vertices and the target may cycle. On the source side, we generalise from a single directed path to a directed acyclic neighbourhood GA swept once in longest-path layer order, with a split-factor recurrence that (i) sums — not minimises — over the branches entering a junction so that every incident edge is covered, and (ii) divides each vertex's accumulated cost equally among its outgoing edges, which counts each vertex's local cost exactly once on a tree-shaped source. Split consistency is then closed during the build, by a forbid-and-rebuild coupling that acts on cells — (source-vertex, target-vertex) pairs — and never on vertices, until every surviving exit of a split is linked by all of its children. The matching itself is extracted afterwards by a cell-level join: a reverse-topological backward sweep that rebuilds per-vertex tables out of the emissions alone (the forward table serves it only as pruning) and returns the cheapest whole-graph row that passes the four validity rules. That extraction is exact over the full space of valid warpings — reconvergent (diamond) sources included, where it matches full-space brute force on 195 of 195 cases — so reconvergence is not, in this design, a place where exactness is surrendered.

1.1 Contributions

  • Graph-DTW: DTW against a directed graph target. A dynamic program that warps one directed A-edge onto a local target graph GB that may contain directed cycles, by replacing DTW's scalar predecessor with a within-row Dijkstra over GB's arcs (so one A-vertex can cover a run of B-vertices and GB can loop). It admits a point emission and a segment emission (middle-to-middle distance plus a λ-weighted bearing term) that pins heading and forbids backward traversal by construction.
  • A projection-based, continuous local-graph builder. A single construction turns both sides into local directed graphs whose vertex pools are enriched by the foot-of-perpendicular projections of the other side's nodes (the projection-pool idea of Keogh & Ratanamahatana, 2004, applied to a graph target), keeps every vertex owned by exactly one edge, and stitches coincident junction endpoints with free directed inter-edge arcs — the structural rule that prevents route collapse and topological U-turns.
  • DAG-DTW: generalising the source from a path to a DAG. A joint solve over a whole acyclic source neighbourhood GA, with a coverage sum over incoming branches (no incident edge can be dropped) and a 1/outdeg split factor that charges each vertex's emission exactly once across all sinks on a tree-shaped source. The sweep is a longest-path layering, not a bare topological sort (Kahn, 1962): a topological order may place a successor of one split child ahead of its sibling, whereas layering completes a split's children as a group before anything downstream of them is filled — which is what makes the coupling below well-posed. Graph-DTW is the single-path special case; the recurrence reduces to DTW's three moves on a chain.
  • The V3 coupling: split consistency closed during the build. Filled independently, two children of a split can link back to different cells of the split — a successor-rule (V3) break the forward sum cannot see, and a phantom no matching realises. A forbid-and-rebuild step closes it in layer order: as each child completes, every cell of the split it does not link to is marked forbidden; a newly forbidden cell is dead for all siblings, so earlier children re-run their whole row; the forbidden set grows monotonically, so the iteration reaches a fixed point in at most |cand(a)| rounds. It acts on cells, never on vertices — hence identical in point and segment mode — and its invariant (check_split_exits) is that every surviving exit of every split is linked by every child.
  • The cell-level join: the extraction. The matching is not traced back through the forward table's frozen argmins; it is re-derived. A reverse-topological backward sweep builds, per source vertex, a table of rows — one row per fully-decided embedding of that vertex's downstream cone into the graph of legal cell moves (cover / stall / advance) — from the emissions alone, the forward table contributing only pruning. Rows are contracted per (entry, pending) interface, which is what keeps a join a join rather than a cross-product of subtree choices; the selection then takes the cheapest root row that the judge accepts. This is a minimum-cost embedding of A into the cell-move graph, not a shortest path (a path cannot fork at a split, nor make two arms agree at a merge), and it is exact over the full space.
  • Validation against a full-space reference. A second, independent extraction — a vertex-level junction join, exact over the family of stored histories — cross-validates the cell-level join, which is itself certified against a full-space brute-force reference (the vertex join is not: on dense-target coverage it can be strictly beaten, a divergence pinned in the suite). Standing numbers: 195/195 exact on reconvergent DAG sources (75 jittered diamonds × 3 weight settings + 120 random reconvergent DAGs); on a structured 384-case envelope the cell join returns 384/384 valid matchings and the vertex join 379/384; the invariant C(cell) ≤ C(vertex join) is pinned in the suite (a violation would be an exactness bug by definition); and match_dag(engine="all") runs both and returns the cheapest valid matching. Every returned matching is finally judged by check_rules — the extraction never returns an invalid one.
  • An honest characterisation of the remaining limit. The residual limitation is not reconvergence but a cyclic target: on a 2-cycle p⇄q the local no-cross predicate (V1) cannot orient a step, so the geometrically correct matching is flagged along with every alternative. This is a property of the validity predicate, not of the matcher: the cell join refuses loudly, the vertex-level join returns the valid stall, and engine="all" still succeeds. Segment-mode DAG-DTW is shipped, not deferred: the identical algorithm runs on the line digraphs L(A), L(B), where the state is a segment pair and the bearing term is always paid.
  • Practical controls. Two weights on the objective: α ∈ (0, 1], a coverage discount on a 1:N cell, and β ∈ [1, ∞), a stall penalty on an N:1 cell, so the matcher spreads the source rather than collapsing it; α = β = 1 (the defaults) reproduces the unweighted cost bit-for-bit. Downstream, the pipeline emits routes_long/routes_summary for graph-DTW and dag_long/dag_summary/dag_parts for DAG-DTW — the last being a per-edge decomposition into match/head/tail parts that reports begin/end non-overlap on both sides.

2 Problem Formulation

Networks. Let A and B be directed road networks modelling the same physical streets. Each is a set of directed edges; a two-way road appears as two opposing directed edges. Every edge carries a polyline geometry, and edges meet at shared junction points. We call A the source (the network to be matched) and B the target.

Local graphs and sampling. Conflation is solved locally, on a neighbourhood at a time. From A we extract a connected source sub-network and represent it as a local directed graph GA whose vertices are geometry samples every Δ metres (step_meters) and whose arcs are the directed sample-to-sample steps; GB is the analogous local graph on the target. The two pipelines join edges differently. In graph-DTW (Section 4) a vertex belongs to exactly one edge, recorded by vert_edge(v), and coincident junction endpoints are joined by free directed inter-edge arcs (edge u's end → edge w's start) that carry connectivity but are not ridable. In DAG-DTW (Section 5) the builder edges_to_digraph densifies each polyline and junction-snaps coincident endpoints into a single shared vertex — a junction is a vertex — so there are no stitch connectors at all. Either way A must be subdivided: at least one interior vertex per edge, which is what makes a split's children pairwise incomparable in the sweep order. For a source vertex a we write Apred(a) / Asucc(a) for its neighbours in GA and outdeg(a) for its out-degree; likewise Bpred(v) / Bsucc(v) on the target. Each source vertex is gated to a candidate set cand(a) = { v : ‖a − v‖ ≤ r } (match_radius_m); a pair (a, v) inside the gate is a cell, and the whole algorithm runs on cells.

The two local graphs differ in an essential, deliberately-exploited asymmetry:

  • GA (source) is required to be a directed acyclic graph — a topologically-orderable branching neighbourhood (a single directed path in the graph-DTW special case). Forks, merges and reconvergences (diamonds, divided carriageways) are all legal; only a directed cycle is rejected (NotADAG).
  • GB (target) may contain directed cycles (roundabouts, divided-carriageway loops). No acyclicity is assumed on the target.

Emission (local cost). The local cost of aligning source vertex a to target vertex v is E(a,v) ≥ 0. In point mode E(a,v) = ‖a − v‖, the Euclidean distance in metres. In segment mode the state is an (A-segment, B-arc) pair (i,e) with e = u→v a forward B-arc, and

(1) E(i,e) = ‖mid(ai,ai+1) − mid(u,v)‖ + λ · dcirc( θ(si), θ(e) ),

the middle-to-middle distance between the source segment si and the target arc plus λ = bearing_weight times the circular heading difference dcirc ∈ [0°, 180°]. The bearing term is what pins heading, since a middle-to-middle distance alone is blind to a segment rotating about its own middle.

Output: a route assignment. A solution is a matching relation M ⊆ V(A) × V(B); for a source vertex a, M(a) = { v : (a,v) ∈ M } is the run of target vertices that a covers — a singleton in the 1:1 case, several consecutive B-vertices where the target is sampled more finely. (A single-label map φ could not express that: 1:N coverage is a run, not a label.) Each source edge's matched route — the connected, direction-respecting sequence of B-edges it traverses — is read off by grouping the runs of its vertices by owning edge. Because GB's arcs are forward-only (and, in graph-DTW, inter-edge stitches are non-ridable connectivity), a route can never ride a B-edge backward, nor U-turn onto a side edge and return. It can, however, hairpin onto an edge's own reverse twin: end(u) → start(twin(u)) is a legitimate head-to-tail arc, since the twin starts exactly where u ends. Measured on Sundbyberg, 18 routes do so (2 at under 5 m drift, passing every threshold). Forbidding it requires a target-side twin map; we state this as a known limitation rather than claim a guarantee the construction does not deliver.

Constraints. M is a valid warping iff it satisfies four rules, all properties of M alone:

  1. (V1) Monotonicity — no cross. ∀ (a,v) ∈ M, ∀ a− ∈ Apred(a), ∀ v+ ∈ Bsucc(v): (a−, v+) ∉ M. A predecessor of a may not sit ahead of a on the target — this is what forbids a backward step.
  2. (V2) Predecessor rule — every cell is fed. Each (a,v) ∈ M either continues a's own run (some v− ∈ Bpred(v) has (a,v−) ∈ M), or every predecessor of a feeds it — each a− ∈ Apred(a) sits on v itself (a stall) or one B-arc behind it (an advance). This is why no incident edge at a merge can be dropped, and why the branch operator in the recurrence is a sum, not a min.
  3. (V3) Successor rule — every cell continues. The mirror of (V2) over Asucc / Bsucc. (V2) keeps a merge from being entered at two points; (V3) keeps a split from being left at two points.
  4. (V4) Full coverage. M(a) ≠ ∅ for every source vertex: nothing is left unmatched. A source's entry into B and a sink's exit from it are free, but interior junctions are pinned — every route through a junction crosses B at the same cell.

The judge check_rules(M, A, B) tests exactly these four rules on the final relation (each restricted to neighbours present in M), on whichever pair of graphs the match lives on — A,B in point mode, L(A),L(B) in segment mode.

Objective. Among valid matchings, minimise total shape drift, each cell's emission charged by the move that enters it:

(2) C(M) = Σ(a,v) ∈ M w(a,v) · E( a, v ),    w = 1 (1:1 advance), α (1:N coverage), β (N:1 stall)

with α ∈ (0, 1] and β ∈ [1, ∞), defaults α = β = 1, which reproduces the unweighted emission sum exactly. Covering a finely-sampled target may be discounted; stacking several source vertices on one target cell is never discounted, only penalised. Reported quality stays the raw Σ(a,v) ∈ M E(a,v) of the chosen matching — the average drift in metres, together with per-edge drift and coverage. It is count-weighted and hence depends on the sampling density Δ; a length-weighted, density-independent objective is composed downstream from the per-part decomposition rather than inside the DP.

We stress a correctness caveat used throughout the paper: the forward dynamic program's own optimum, Σsinks t minv D[t][v] (where D is the forward cost table introduced in the next section), coincides with C(M) only when GA is a tree. On a reconvergent diamond the branches can share an ancestor, so the forward sum double-counts it: the quantity is then neither the realized cost nor a bound on it. The realized cost C(M) of the extracted relation is the definition of solution quality; the forward optimum is a diagnostic, not the objective — and it is precisely because the extraction of Section 8 (Appendix B) never reads D's values that its exactness survives reconvergence.

Junction consistency vs. per-edge optimality (the crux). Consider two routes s1→t1 and s2→t2 through a common junction j. Optimised independently, route 1 may prefer j at one target vertex and route 2 at another; but j is a single vertex and (V4) pins it to a single cell. Forcing one route's preference onto j pushes the other into a backward step, even when every A-edge is matched to the geometrically correct B-edge. Hence the objective above cannot be minimised edge-by-edge: junction consistency couples all edges incident to j, and a joint solve over the source neighbourhood is required. The mechanism by which the algorithms of this paper enforce that coupling — the split-factor recurrence, the V3 forbid-and-rebuild coupling, and the cell-level join extraction — is the subject of the sections that follow.

3 Related Work

Our method sits at the intersection of four largely separate literatures: (i) probabilistic matching of a GPS trajectory to a road map, (ii) geometric curve matching under the Fréchet distance, (iii) dynamic time warping (DTW) for elastic sequence alignment, and (iv) map conflation / multi-representation matching in GIS. It also borrows inference machinery from graphical models. We review each family, then state precisely how network-to-network, directed, junction-consistent, segmentation-robust shape conflation differs from all of them. Throughout, A denotes the source network and B the target, both directed (Section 2).

3.1 GPS-trajectory-to-map matching

The dominant paradigm for online navigation and floating-car data is matching a noisy point sequence (a GPS track) to the edges of a known road graph. Early geometric and topological methods score candidate edges by proximity and heading and grow a matched path incrementally (White et al., 2000); the survey of Quddus et al. (2007) catalogues this generation. The field then consolidated around a hidden Markov model (HMM): each GPS sample is an observation, each nearby road edge a hidden state, emission probabilities encode measurement noise, transition probabilities encode routing plausibility (great-circle vs. on-road distance), and the Viterbi algorithm recovers the maximum-likelihood edge sequence (Newson & Krumm, 2009). This framing is robust to noise and sparse sampling and is now standard.

The structural difference from our setting is the source. In HMM map matching the source is an unstructured point cloud with no topology of its own: it never branches, never merges, and imposes no junction constraints. The hidden chain is a path, so Viterbi — a one-dimensional DP — suffices. In network-to-network conflation the source A is itself a directed graph with its own vertices, edges, out-/in-degrees and junctions, and the requirement is that source edges meeting at a junction agree on where that junction lands in B. That is a constraint across several simultaneously-matched paths, not along one. Our coupled forward table and cell-level join extraction (Sections 5–8) generalise the Viterbi idea from a chain to a DAG source — the backtrack becomes a reverse-topological table join, because a DAG's optimum cannot be read off one argmin per state — and our horizontal Dijkstra move over GB generalises the transition step to absorb the different segmentation between A and B (one A-vertex may ride a run of B-vertices) rather than treating each edge as a single discrete state.

3.2 Curve and map matching via the Fréchet distance

A second family measures the similarity of two polylines directly. The Fréchet distance — the classic "dog-walking" metric — and the free-space diagram of Alt & Godau (1995) give an exact, order-preserving notion of curve similarity and an O(mn log mn) decision procedure. Brakatsoulas et al. (2005) apply both the Fréchet and a weaker average Fréchet distance to match a vehicle trajectory against road-network polylines, making the geometry (not a probabilistic emission) the primary signal.

This family is the closest in spirit to ours: it is shape-first and reparametrisation-invariant, exactly the property needed when A and B sample the same physical road at different vertex densities. But the standard formulation matches one curve to one curve (or to individually-enumerated network paths); it is undirected in its basic form, carries no explicit heading term, and offers no mechanism for a shared junction label when several curves branch off a common point. Our emission is a DTW-style alignment cost rather than a bottleneck (max) distance, which lets us (a) sum a total shape-drift objective C(M) (equation (2)) that is comparable across edges and (b) add the middle-to-middle + λ·Δbearing segment cost that pins direction — the term that distinguishes a road from its anti-parallel twin. Where Fréchet matching answers "how far apart are these two curves," we answer "which connected, directed route of B-edges does each A-edge correspond to, consistently at junctions."

3.3 Dynamic time warping

The elastic-alignment engine we build on is DTW, introduced for spoken-word recognition by Sakoe & Chiba (1978), who also gave the band constraint we reuse to restrict B-candidates (Section 2). Berndt & Clifford (1994) established DTW as a general time-series pattern-matching tool. Classical DTW aligns two 1-D sequences on a rectangular cost grid with three moves (vertical / horizontal / diagonal). Our contribution is to keep the three-move recurrence but replace one sequence axis with a directed graph: the horizontal move becomes a Dijkstra shortest path over GB (whose arcs may form cycles, e.g. roundabouts), and — in DAG-DTW — the other axis is generalised from a path to a layered source DAG with a 1/outdeg split factor across branches. To our knowledge, DTW with a directed-graph alignment target, swept jointly over a branching source, is the novel core of this work. The continuous projection-pool construction of the local graphs follows the "projection" refinement of DTW popularised by Keogh and Ratanamahatana (2004), adapted here to a graph target so that every foot-of-perpendicular projection of one network's nodes onto the other becomes an alignable vertex.

3.4 Map conflation and multi-representation matching

The GIS conflation literature is where our problem — reconciling two independently-digitised maps of the same area — originates, going back to Saalfeld's (1988) formalisation of automated map compilation as node/arc correspondence with iterative rubber-sheeting. A large body of work matches street datasets across representations using buffer-growing, node-degree and topological signatures, and geometric attributes, often iteratively (Volz, 2006). These methods are explicitly network-to-network and address different segmentation, but they typically operate on undirected feature geometry, decide correspondence with buffer-overlap or node-matching heuristics rather than an optimal shape-alignment DP, and enforce junction agreement only indirectly (through node matching), without a single joint objective. Our method contributes an optimisation view of the same task: a shape-drift DP whose optimum is the matched route set, that is directed by construction (a source edge running against a target edge matches that target's reverse twin rather than reversing along it), and whose junctions are resolved jointly — pinned by the split coupling during the build and by the cell-level join at extraction — rather than by independent per-node matches.

3.5 Inference tools we borrow

The vertex-level junction join we keep as a cross-validation engine (Appendix B) is best read as standard graphical-model inference: its per-junction tables and their summation at a split are min-sum (max-product) belief propagation, which is exact on trees and polytrees (Pearl, 1988) — the same principle that underlies Viterbi decoding. That is also the limit of the analogy, and the reason it is a cross-check rather than the engine of record: message passing on a graph with an undirected cycle (the "diamond," where two directed paths from a common ancestor re-meet) has no such guarantee, and the tables it passes are vertex labels with frozen histories, which cannot express where a coverage run starts. The shipped engine therefore does not condition on loops at all. The cell-level join of Section 8 optimises over the full cell-move space, reconvergent sources included, and its optimality is established not by an appeal to tree structure but by certification against a full-space brute-force reference (195/195 exact on reconvergent DAG sources, Section 9). Earlier iterations of this work did use cutset-conditioning reference solvers (in the spirit of Dechter's, 1990, cutset decomposition) to certify optimality on reconvergent sources; the cell-level join made them unnecessary and they are no longer part of the design.

4 Graph-DTW: dynamic time warping to a directed graph

4.1 From a scalar time axis to a directed graph

Classical dynamic time warping (DTW) aligns two sequences by a monotone warping path through a cost grid, using three local moves — advance the first sequence, advance the second, or advance both (Sakoe & Chiba, 1978; Berndt & Clifford, 1994). Its second axis is a total order: at step j the only predecessor is j−1. Graph-DTW retains DTW's move set but replaces that scalar axis with a directed target graph GB, so that the predecessor of a B-vertex v is any graph-predecessor u ∈ pred(v). The source remains a single directed path: one A-edge, densified into vertices a0,…,aN−1 at resolution Δ (step_meters). Both axes are laid out by the same projection-based, continuous pool constructor (_node_projection_pool), which augments a line's vertex pool with the feet of perpendicular projections of the opposite side's nodes and then gap-fills evenly, spreading samples across any gap wider than Δ instead of stepping from one end and leaving a leftover sliver; the target side is then assembled into the directed graph GB (build_local_digraph), where every vertex is owned by exactly one edge and coincident junction endpoints are kept as separate coincident vertices stitched by directed inter-edge arcs. The construction is detailed in Appendix A.

Let D[i][v] denote the minimum cost to align the source prefix a0…ai so that ai is labelled by B-vertex v. With the point-mode emission E(a,v) = ‖a−v‖, the recurrence is DTW's three moves with j−1 generalised to pred(v):

(3) D[i][v] = E(ai,v) + min( D[i−1][v] (vertical), minu∈pred(v)D[i][u] (horizontal), minu∈pred(v)D[i−1][u] (diagonal) )

The vertical move advances A while holding the B-position (source denser than target); the diagonal advances both; the horizontal advances B while holding ai (target denser than source, so one A-vertex rides a run of B-vertices). Entry is free, D[0][v] = E(a0,v) for every v, and termination takes minv D[N−1][v]: the warping path always spans the entire A-edge, but is free to begin and end anywhere in GB.

4.2 The horizontal move is a Dijkstra pass

The one structural subtlety is that the horizontal term self-references D[i][·] within the same row i. In classical DTW this is harmless — the second axis is acyclic, so a left-to-right sweep suffices. Here GB models real road topology and may contain directed cycles (roundabouts, loops), so no topological sweep of row i exists. Because every emission is non-negative, however, the within-row problem is exactly a non-negative-weight shortest-path problem and is solved exactly by Dijkstra's algorithm (Dijkstra, 1959) over the arcs of GB. Concretely, first form the row base from the two moves that read the finalised row i−1,

(4) base[v] = E(ai,v) + min( D[i−1][v], minu∈pred(v)D[i−1][u] )

then run Dijkstra, relaxing along each arc u→w of GB and re-paying the emission on arrival:

(5) D[i][w] ← min( D[i][w], D[i][u] + E(ai,w) )

Re-paying E(ai,w) on every relaxation is what lets a single A-vertex ride an arbitrarily long run of B-vertices at its own local cost, and keeps every horizontal edge weight non-negative so Dijkstra remains exact on a cyclic GB. The cost per A-edge is O(N · |EB| log |VB|); the local graphs are small. Equations (3)–(5) are the α = β = 1 default of the same step-weighted family DAG-DTW uses (§8.3, Appendix A): graph_dtw_align also accepts a coverage discount α ∈ (0,1] on the horizontal move and a stall penalty β ≥ 1 on the vertical one, and at the defaults the weighted recurrence reproduces the unweighted one above byte-for-byte.

4.3 Backtracking to a route

Backtracking the argmins of (3) yields a vertex-to-vertex label map φ; grouping consecutive steps by their owning B-edge (vert_edge[v]) turns it into an ordered route of B-edges. Because the builder emits only forward arcs — intra-edge arcs run along the digitized direction only (a bidirectional road is already two opposing directed edges), and an inter-edge junction stitch joins one edge's end to another edge's start, and nothing else, wherever the two coincide within snap_tolerance_m (default 0.75 m) — the recovered route is directed and connected by construction: it may flow through a junction, but it can neither reverse, nor U-turn onto a side edge and return, nor hop onto a geometrically close but topologically disconnected parallel road. Leading and trailing zero-traversal touches, where the source merely grazes a junction vertex without walking the edge, are trimmed; if nothing is traversed the edge returns NO_MATCH.

4.4 Segment and bearing emission

Point mode scores a vertex against a vertex and is blind to orientation. The segment emission (_segment_dp_pairs) instead lifts the DP state to a pair (A-segment, B-arc): state (i,e) means "A-segment si = ai→ai+1 rides forward B-arc e = u→v". Its per-state cost is a middle-to-middle distance plus an optional heading penalty,

(6) E(i,e) = ‖mid(ai,ai+1) − mid(u,v)‖ + λ · dcirc( θ(si), θ(e) )

where θ(·) is a heading, dcirc ∈ [0°,180°] is the circular heading difference, and λ = bearing_weight. The three moves carry over unchanged — A-advance on the same arc (N:1), arc-advance within a row via the same Dijkstra (1:N), or both — and the arc-state optimum is emitted back as vertex pairs, so all downstream grouping, trimming and metrics are shared with point mode. It is here, and only here, that the junction stitches become free: an arc shorter than 0.5 m is flagged non-ridable — pure connectivity, zero emission, never an (A-segment, B-arc) state of its own, crossable only by passing through it within a row. The bearing term is necessary because a middle-to-middle distance alone is invariant to a segment rotating about its own midpoint; λ pins heading, with λ ∈ [1,5] a good working range. Two earlier emission variants (bearing charged only on diagonal moves, which let the DP escape the heading penalty through stalls, and an endpoint-average interim distance) were superseded by (6); emission="midpoint" is a deprecated alias for "segment".

5 DAG-DTW: generalising the source to a DAG

5.1 Motivation and the design asymmetry

Graph-DTW matches one A-edge at a time. But road networks branch, and A-edges meeting at a junction should agree on where that junction lands in B. DAG-DTW therefore promotes the source from a single path to a whole connected acyclic subgraph GA — a junction neighbourhood or branching corridor — matched in one solve (match_dag). The design rests on a deliberate asymmetry between the two sides:

  • The source GA must be a DAG. Forks, merges and reconvergences (diamonds, divided carriageways) are all legal; only a directed cycle is rejected (NotADAG). Acyclicity is what buys a build order in which every predecessor's row is final when a vertex is reached (§5.3).
  • The source must be subdivided — at least one interior vertex on every real edge (the densification of edges_to_digraph, at step_meters). This is what makes a split's children pairwise incomparable in the build order and a merge's parents childless siblings; both the ordering and the coupling of §6.2 rest on it.
  • The target GB may cycle. The coverage move over GB is not a sweep but a within-row fixed point, relaxed to convergence (§5.3), so roundabouts and loops in the target need no special case.

A junction is a vertex on both sides. A source vertex a paired with a candidate target vertex v is a cell — the object the whole algorithm runs on. Candidates are radius-gated, cand(a) = {v : ‖a−v‖ ≤ r} (match_radius_m; if fewer than k_min target vertices fall inside the gate, the k_min nearest are kept anyway), and each A-vertex's cells are stored as one row on its node. A pair pruned by the gate therefore needs no sentinel — absence is the ∞ cell — and the table is O(|A| · band) rather than O(|A| · |VB|).

5.2 The objective, the recurrence, and the split factor

The output is not a label map but a matching relation M ⊆ V(A) × V(B): M(a) = {v : (a,v) ∈ M} is the run of target vertices that a covers (a singleton in the 1:1 case). Its cost charges every matched cell by the move that enters it — weight 1 on a 1:1 advance, β on an N:1 stall, α on a 1:N coverage cell:

(7) minimize   C(M) = Σ(a,v)∈M w(a,v) · E(a,v)    subject to M a valid warping (V1)–(V4)

The four rules are properties of M alone: (V1) monotonicity — no crossing step, so no predecessor of a may sit on a successor of v; (V2) every matched cell is fed — it continues its own run or every A-predecessor feeds it; (V3) the mirror over successors — every matched cell continues; (V4) full coverage — M(a) ≠ ∅ for every a, with a source's entry and a sink's exit free and interior junctions pinned. They are tested, on the final relation, by check_rules — the judge (§6.4).

The forward table is the first half of the solve. Let D[a][v] be the minimum cost of matching a's upstream cone with a pinned at v. Each A-predecessor p has two ways into v — stepp = minx∈Bpred(v) D[p][x] (it advances one B-arc) and stallp = D[p][v] (it is already on v) — and the recurrence is a three-way min over the move that enters the cell:

(8) D[a][v] = min {
  E(a,v) + Σp∈Apred(a) steppoutdeg(p) (D) every branch advances — full E
  β E(a,v) + minq [ stallqoutdeg(q) + Σp≠q min(stallp, stepp)outdeg(p) ] (V) ≥ 1 branch stalls — N:1
  α E(a,v) + minv′∈Bpred(v)D[a][v′] (H) 1:N coverage along B }

Four features carry the DAG:

Sum over source-predecessors (coverage). At a merge the costs of all incoming A-branches are added, not minimised: every approach must be aligned, none discarded, and the sum is finite only if every branch reaches v — this is (V2) folded into the cost. The rule is sum over branches, min over B-positions; a min in place of the sum would leave one branch unmatched.

The 1/outdeg(p) split factor. Each vertex divides its accumulated cost equally among its outgoing A-edges, so a point shared by several downstream branches is counted once when those branches are summed again — on a tree, where the branches above a merge live in disjoint subtrees. §8.2 gives the worked Y-split. On a reconvergent DAG two branches can share an ancestor, so the forward sum double-counts it: D's conservation argument is tree-only, which is why the exactness claim on reconvergent sources belongs to the extraction and not to this table (§7.1).

The forced stall. The inner minq of the (V) case requires some branch to genuinely stall, so the penalty β is never charged on a pure advance. At α = β = 1 the weighted recurrence reproduces the unweighted cost bit-for-bit (§8.3).

Back-pointers are cells. bpD[a][v] records exactly the cells the value came from — the classic DTW arrow generalised to a list: empty at a source (free entry), one pair per predecessor on an advance or a stall (a merge's coupling in one list), and the same-vertex pair (a,v′) on a coverage step. The move type is read off whose vertex appears; no tags. Every neighbour look-up in (8) skips cells flagged forbidden by the coupling of §6.2.

5.3 Reductions and computation

The recurrence contains graph-DTW as a special case, with no branch logic:

  • Chain (|Apred(a)| = 1, outdeg = 1): the split factor is 1, the sum has one term, and at α = β = 1 the three cases collapse to E(a,v) + min( D[a][v′], D[a′][v], D[a′][v′] ) — exactly graph-DTW's three moves (3).
  • Source (Apred(a) = ∅): the empty sum gives D[a][v] = E(a,v) — free entry at every source; a sink's exit is likewise free. DAG-DTW is thus a strict superset of graph-DTW: a single-path source is graph-DTW.

Computation is forward(A, B, α, β): one sweep of GA, one row filled per vertex. The order is not a plain topological sort. The build must complete every split's children as a group before anything downstream of them is filled, so that the group can be reconciled (§6.2) while nothing has yet read it — and J, b₁, x, b₂ is a perfectly legal topological order that nevertheless places x, a successor of b1, before its sibling b2. Longest-path layering (layer_order) does guarantee it: a source gets L = 0 and every other vertex L(v) = maxp∈Apred(v) L(p) + 1; vertices are taken in ascending L (ties by id, for determinism). L strictly increases along every edge, so the order is topological; on a subdivided source a split's children share the layer L(split)+1, ahead of all of their successors, and a merge lands after all of its branches.

The (H) term is the one structural subtlety, as it was in graph-DTW (§4.2): it reads other cells of the same row. B carries no order of its own — it may cycle — so the row is relaxed until nothing changes, lowering D and repointing bpD in the same step, so the pointer always names the cell that produced the value. Because αE ≥ 0, this is a monotone descent to the unique least fixed point, correct even on a cyclic B where a single pass would leave cells un-relaxed. (Mode 1 does the same job with a within-row Dijkstra; the DAG pass carries no heap — one fixed-point relaxation covers every α ∈ (0,1] and every B.) Every argmin breaks exact-cost ties by one fixed total order on B's vertices, so the tables are invariant to B's insertion order.

Both modes share all of this: point mode runs on A and B; segment mode runs the identical code on the directed line graphs L(A), L(B), whose nodes are the arcs, carrying midpoint and bearing (§7.4).

6 Junction consistency

6.1 Why independently filled branches fail

The recurrence of (8) fills each branch of a split independently, and nothing in it forces the branches to agree on where the split itself lands. Consider two routes s→t1 and s→t2 through a junction j. Optimised in isolation, branch 1 may want j at one B-vertex and branch 2 at another — but j is one vertex and its run is one run. Summing such per-branch minima puts a single source vertex in two B-places at once: a phantom that no matching realizes, and, if one label is later forced onto the other branch, a non-forward B-transition on a GA arc — a backward step. Crucially this occurs even when every A-edge maps to the correct B-edge: the error is in the shared junction cell, not the topology. It is a (V3) break — the split's cell is left in two different ways — and the forward sum cannot see it, because each branch's own arithmetic is impeccable.

6.2 The V3 coupling: forbid-and-rebuild

The shipped fix closes the break during the build, in the layer order of §5.3, and it acts on cells, never on vertices — so it is identical in point and segment mode and indifferent to how many parents a child has. Each cell carries a forbidden flag: once set, no back-pointer may link to that cell — every place the recurrence reads a neighbour cell (advance source, stall source, same-row coverage source) skips it, though the cell's own value is still computed. Per split a with children a1, a2, … (one layer):

  1. Build each child's row with (8), skipping forbidden cells.
  2. Forbid non-shared exits. As each child completes — including the first — mark forbidden every cell of a that it does not link to. The surviving exits are the intersection of what the children actually used:
(9) Allowed(a) = ∩i links(ai),    forbidden(a) = cand(a) \ Allowed(a)
  1. Rebuild whole rows. A newly-forbidden cell is dead for all siblings, past and future; every earlier child that had linked it re-runs its entire row under the current flags — the within-row coverage chains of (H) make a single-cell patch impossible.
  2. Iterate to the fixed point. A rebuilt row may re-link to an exit some sibling does not share; the forbidden set only grows, so the loop terminates in at most |cand(a)| rounds.

A worked trace, split a with children a1, a2 and cand(a) = {v1, v2, v3}: a1 links {v1, v2}, forbidding v3; a2 then links only {v1}, forbidding v2; because a1 had leaned on v2, its whole row is rebuilt under the current flags and re-links through v1 — fixed point, Allowed(a) = {v1}. At the fixed point every surviving exit of every split is linked by all of its children, so each surviving option is (V3)-valid; keeping several is legitimate — the extraction (§8.1) chooses among them, and never commits a vertex to a forbidden cell. check_split_exits is the invariant. If a split's exits empty out there is no (V3)-valid warping inside the gate, and the pass raises a feasibility error naming the vertex (increase match_radius_m) rather than returning a broken match.

6.3 An earlier iteration: forward–backward and the guarded backtrack

The coupling superseded an earlier design that resolved junctions after the fact, and it is worth recording why. A backward table B[a][v] — the same recurrence with both graphs reversed (sum over successors, split factor 1/indeg, reverse sweep) — gives the cheapest cost from a to the sinks with a at v, and the two tables were combined at each junction by a joint argmin, φ(j) = argminv ( D[j][v] + B[j][v] − E(j,v) ), the −E removing the double count of j's own emission; the label was then committed in a reverse-topological backtrack that took the cheapest v still forward-reaching every already-fixed successor's label. That is min-sum belief propagation with a reachability guard — exact on trees (Pearl, 1988), and appealing. It was superseded for two reasons: the joint seed was proven unsound at a merge, and, pinning vertices, it can say nothing about where inside a run a child should connect. Both tables survive in the code strictly as diagnostics: backward fills the mirror table, extract_two_table is the legacy two-table traceback over the two pointer sets, and check_reciprocity is the cross-table agreement check — on a committed matching, every source edge the forward pointers thread must be threaded back identically by the backward pointers. It holds on the optimum only: away from it the two tables optimise differently-pinned subproblems and legitimately disagree. Appendix B records the design history.

6.4 What is guaranteed, and by what

Each rule has an owner. (V2) is enforced by the forward sum — a merge's cost is infinite unless every branch reaches the cell. (V3) is enforced by the coupling of §6.2, whose invariant is check_split_exits. (V4) is enforced by the extraction, which embeds every source vertex by construction (§8.1). And all four are re-checked, on the final relation, by check_rules(M, src, tgt) — the judge — on whichever graph the match lives on (A,B or L(A),L(B)), each rule restricted to the neighbours present in M. The judge's word is final: candidate solutions that violate a rule are skipped, and if none survives the call raises rather than returning an invalid matching.

Consistency, however, is not optimality. The coupled table decides consistently, but it still carries the 1/outdeg fractions whose conservation argument is tree-only. Exactness on a reconvergent source is a claim about the extraction, not about the table — which is the subject of the next section.

7 Reconvergence, exactness, and the segment lift

7.1 The diamond, and why the forward table cannot claim it

An undirected cycle in a DAG occurs exactly where two directed paths from a common ancestor re-meet: a diamond, split j1 → {up, down} → merge j2 — a divided carriageway, a slip road rejoining. These are accepted by default; only a directed cycle is rejected. But they break a premise. On a tree, a merge's incoming branches live in disjoint upstream subtrees — if they shared an ancestor, that ancestor would fork and rejoin, which is precisely an undirected cycle a tree forbids. That disjointness is what licenses adding branch costs at a merge, and it is what makes the 1/outdeg split exact: the shared prefix is divided among the branches and reassembled exactly once. On a diamond the branches do share an ancestor, so the forward sum double-counts it. D's exactness argument is therefore tree-only, and any extraction that consumes D's values inherits that limit (§8.4) — the same reason min-sum message passing is exact only on graphs with no undirected cycle (Pearl, 1988): the two arms constrain j1 and j2 simultaneously, in both directions.

7.2 The cell-level join is exact there

The engine of record never reads those values. The cell-level join (extract_cell, §8.1) builds its tables backward from the sinks out of the emissions alone; the forward table serves only as pruning — a forbidden or infinite cell can appear in no row — and no 1/outdeg fraction enters its ledger, which pays each cell exactly once, at 1, β, or α. There is consequently no tree premise left to break: a diamond's shared ancestor is reconciled by the very mechanism that reconciles a merge — the two arms carry the shared vertex's entry cell as a pending obligation and must present the same cell when they meet at their common ancestor's join, or the combination dies (§8.1).

This is verified rather than asserted. Against a full-space brute force — every entry cell and every run combination enumerated — on reconvergent sources — 75 jittered-diamond cases spanning three weight settings, plus 120 random subdivided reconvergent DAGs, 195 cases in all — the cell-level join is exact and valid on 195/195. The claim on reconvergent sources is the cell join's alone: the vertex-level cross-check of §8.4 consumes D and is judged, not trusted, there.

7.3 Design history: exact conditioning

Before the cell-level join existed, the diamond was attacked by exact conditioning: condition on a small cut of junction labels, enumerate the cut's candidates, solve the now loop-free remainder exactly by min-sum belief propagation, and cross-check a recursive-cut solver against a one-shot feedback-vertex-set enumeration. That study did its job — it confirmed the diagnosis that the failure lay in the shared junction cells rather than in the branch routing, and its mutual-agreement harness localised two real correctness bugs in the surrounding code, both triggered by anti-parallel B geometry (normal in real conflation). It is not part of the shipped library: the exactness that conditioning bought at the cost of enumeration is now had by construction, over a strictly larger space — runs included — from the cell-level join. Appendix C keeps the account as design history.

7.4 Segment mode, and the standing limit

Segment mode is shipped, and it is what the DuckDB pipeline runs. A point-mode state is a vertex pair, and dressing its cost with a heading term does not make it segment matching: on an N:1 stall there is no target segment at all, and the heading is silently free. True segment matching makes the state a segment pair — so the identical algorithm runs on the directed line graphs L(A) = line_digraph(A) and L(B), whose nodes are the arcs, each carrying its midpoint as coordinates and its bearing, under the midpoint-plus-bearing emission (6) of §4.4. Layering, the recurrence, the coupling, the extraction and the judge are byte-for-byte the point-mode code on the lifted graphs; every state pays its emission (a stall costs βE, never zero — the heading cannot be bypassed). A junction is a vertex, so the line graph connects real segments directly and there are no stitch connectors to park on; a merge+split vertex becomes a bipartite cluster in L(A) — a reconvergent DAG, accepted natively, on which both engines return valid and agreeing matchings. The matching is emitted and validated on the arcs, M ⊆ E(A) × E(B); any per-point view is derived convenience, never validated in its place.

The standing limit sits on the target side, and it is a limit of the validator, not of the matcher. On a 2-cycle p ⇄ q the local (V1) predicate cannot orient a step: on the smallest such case — a two-vertex source chain over p ⇄ q — the geometrically correct matching, and every separating alternative in either direction, is flagged. There the cell join refuses loudly (its root contraction leaves only the flagged row) while the vertex-level join returns the valid stall, so engine="all" still succeeds; the behaviour is pinned by test (test_two_cycle_judge_prefers_valid). A cyclic B is otherwise unremarkable: the radius gate cuts any roundabout wider than 2r, and the coverage relaxation of §5.3 is a fixed point that converges regardless.

8 Extraction, cost, and the weights

8.1 The extraction: a cell-level join

The extraction is a join over tables built backward from the sinks — not a search, and not a traceback of the forward pointers. Its space is the cell-move graph: the nodes are the cells (a,v), and the edges are the three moves of (8), read as cell transitions:

(10) cover (a,v) → (a,w), w ∈ Bsucc(v)   |   stall (p,v) → (c,v), c ∈ Asucc(p)   |   advance (p,v) → (c,w), c ∈ Asucc(p), w ∈ Bsucc(v)

Stall and advance edges strictly increase A's layer, and a cover edge never leaves its row, so every directed cycle of this graph is confined to a single row — and exists only where a whole B-cycle fits inside one radius gate. A matching is an embedding of A into this graph: each vertex maps to a cover path — its run M(a) — and each A-edge is realized by exactly one stall or advance edge, from the parent's run end to the child's run entry. (V1)–(V4) say precisely that such an embedding exists and covers every vertex, and C(M) charges each cell by the edge that enters it. That is a minimum-cost embedding problem, not a shortest path — a path cannot fork at a split, nor make two arms agree at a merge — which is why the extraction is a dynamic program over per-vertex tables rather than a graph search.

extract_cell sweeps A in reverse topological order, sinks first, giving each vertex X a table of rows (entry, value, pending, cells). entry is the single cell of X exposed to X's parent — the start of X's run; everything else below X is already decided. value is what that cone costs under the ledger below. pending lists the merges below that this line has touched: for each, the entry cell the other arms must present (or the combination dies) and a flag recording whether some arm stalled onto it. cells is the embedding itself — every decided vertex mapped to its run — so M travels with the row: the winning row's cells map is the relation, with no traceback, no reconstruction and no gap-fill. The ledger pays each cell's emission exactly once, weighted by the move that enters it:

(11) C(M) = Σa∈V(A) [ win(a) · E(a, entry(a)) + α Σv∈M(a) \ {entry(a)} E(a,v) ],    win(a) = { 1 on an advance (or a source's free entry); β on a stall }

No fractional factors appear anywhere — plain sums at a split, consumed-once at a merge — and a vertex's own entry-E is deferred to the step that connects it to its parent, because whether it costs 1 or β is the parent's choice of edge. The invariant that keeps this a join rather than a cross-product of subtree choices: once a vertex is processed, its table holds, per (entry, pending) interface, only the cheapest embedding of its cone with that interface — two rows agreeing on the interface are interchangeable to everything upstream.

Worked, on the split A: 0→1, 1→{2,3} over B: s→j, j→{u,d}, every B-vertex 0.5 m off its A-twin, swept 2, 3, 1, 0. The sinks open rows (u, 0, {}, {2:(u)}) and (d, 0, {}, {3:(d)}) — nothing below, nothing paid yet. At vertex 1, entry j with the run (j) ends at j, and both children connect by an advance out of that same run end — j→u and j→d — paying 1·E(2,u) + 1·E(3,d) = 1.0; that per-cell agreement of the two children is (V3), by construction. At vertex 0, child 1 connects by the advance s→j for 1·E(1,j), giving 1.5; the root join then pays 0's own free entry E(0,s) — value 2.0 = C(M) exactly, and self-checked as such. Selection is exactly that: the root rows are tried cheapest first, and the first whose relation passes the judge is returned, with committed[a] = the first cell of a's run. A table that would exceed max_rows raises; it is never silently truncated. Infeasibility is located and loud: a vertex with no surviving cell, or a root join with no valid row, raises a ValueError naming the vertex and telling the caller to increase match_radius_m.

8.2 The realised-cost objective

The reported cost is the cost of the relation — C(M) of (7), computed directly on M, never read off a table — and the joined value of the winning row is asserted equal to it rather than trusted. The headline quality metric is the raw drift: the unweighted mean emission over the matched pairs,

(12) drift(M) = 1|M| Σ(a,v)∈M E(a,v)

comparable to graph-DTW's mean matched distance, and deliberately unweighted: α and β steer which M is selected (§8.3) without inflating or discounting what is then reported about it. In segment mode the pipeline reports it as a pure-geometry mean midpoint distance, with the circular bearing difference carried alongside rather than mixed into it (§8.4).

On a clean tree the forward table's own optimum coincides with the realised total, which makes the split factor's bookkeeping checkable by hand. For a Y-split a0→a1, a1→{a2,a3} with every vertex drifting 0.2 m and outdeg(a1) = 2:

D[a0] = 0.2,    D[a1] = 0.4,    D[a2] = 0.2 + ½ · 0.4 = 0.4,    D[a3] = 0.4

so Σsinks {a2,a3} = 0.8 = 0.2 · 4, matching the four-vertex realised drift; a naive sum without the 1/outdeg split would double-count a0 and a1 and give 1.2 — wrong. The illustration is exactly as tree-only as the argument behind it (§7.1): it is why the shipped extraction re-derives its own value from the emissions and pays each cell once (11), and why value == C(M) is a self-check rather than an assumption. C(M) is count-weighted, hence density-dependent through step_meters; a length-weighted, density-independent objective is future work.

8.3 The coverage weight α and the stall weight β

The plain coverage move re-pays the emission at every covered B-vertex (§4.2), so a 1:N coverage costs linearly in B's sampling density — an artefact of Δ, not of geometry. The weight α = alpha ∈ (0,1] discounts it, and the discount falls on the emission, never on the carried cost: multiplying the carried cost would make the coverage edge weight negative for α < 1 and let the table "launder" cost by wandering through cheap B-vertices; that variant was rejected. Unrolling a coverage v0 → … → vk at drift δ, the first cell is entered by an advance at full δ and each of the remaining k = N−1 by a discounted coverage step, so the run costs

(13) δ ( 1 + α k )

Thus α = 1 reproduces the per-B-point charge bit-for-bit, while α → 0 charges a coverage essentially once, sampling-independently (at δ = 0.4 m and N = 30: 12.0 m at α = 1, falling towards 0.4 m as α → 0). Because αE ≥ 0 remains a non-negative per-step emission, D never decreases along a coverage — no laundering — and the within-row relaxation of §5.3 stays a monotone descent to the least fixed point at every α, on a cyclic B included.

Its companion is the stall weight β = beta ∈ [1,∞) on the N:1 move. Stacking several source vertices onto one target cell is never discounted — only penalized — so the matcher spreads the source along B rather than collapsing it onto a single cell. The asymmetry is the point: α ≤ 1 ≤ β. Both weights live inside the decision cost, so they shift which M is selected — toward more 1:N coverage, away from N:1 compression — while the reported drift (12) stays the raw ΣE of that M. The defaults α = β = 1 reproduce the unweighted cost bit-for-bit, in the recurrence (8) and in the ledger (11) alike.

Why two weights and not three. The ledger has three move types, so it is natural to ask why only two of them carry a multiplier — why the advance is pinned at 1 rather than given a weight γ of its own. Because such a γ would be redundant. Write the objective by move type, with Sadv, Sstall, Scov the sums of E over the cells entered by an advance (or a source's free entry), by a stall, and by a coverage step:

C(M; γ, β, α) = γ·Sadv + β·Sstall + α·Scov = γ · C(M; 1, β/γ, α/γ)    for every γ > 0.

The objective is positively homogeneous in the weight vector, and scaling an objective by a positive constant cannot change which M minimises it. So the weighted family has two degrees of freedom, not three: only the ratios of the stall and coverage weights to the advance weight are observable, and fixing the advance at 1 is a gauge choice, not a restriction. It is also what gives the domains their meaning — α ≤ 1 ≤ β is a statement about ratios to an advance, and it is the reason the pair (α, β) is the whole story. (Pinned in the suite: the extraction returns the same relation for (γ, β, α) as for (1, β/γ, α/γ).)

Two things a third weight could genuinely buy, and why we do not ship them. A source's free entry currently shares the advance's weight of 1, yet it is a different move — there is no predecessor to advance from — so giving it its own multiplier is a real, if narrow, degree of freedom: it would tune where a source root is allowed to enter the target, and it touches one cell per root. We leave it at 1, and say so, rather than carry a parameter whose only effect is at the boundary.

The generalisation the objective actually wants is not another multiplier but a change of measure. The cost sums over matched pairs, so it counts vertices — and a vertex count is a property of the other cartographer's discretisation, not of the road. Charge instead by arc length: let each move pay its emission times the length it accounts for, an advance E·(δa + δb), a coverage step E·δb, a stall E·δa — the discrete form of ∫ E (dsA + dsB). Refine the target and each cell's δb shrinks in proportion, so the total does not move: density-independence by construction rather than by tuning.

This is not α = 0 in disguise — it is its opposite, and the distinction is the whole point. α is a discount; a length is a measure. A discount can be set to zero, and then the move becomes free: at α = β = 0 a stall costs nothing, so the engine piles all eleven source vertices of §9.5 onto a single target cell (C = 9.01, the cheapest possible garbage). A measure cannot be zero, because the road still has length: the arc-length ledger charges a stall by the metres of source it consumes and a coverage step by the metres of target it rides, and nothing is ever free. What makes the per-cell weights small on a finely-drawn target is that each cell is short — and there are proportionally more of them, so the sum does not move. Covering five metres of road costs 2.50 whether the target is drawn every metre or every five centimetres; under a constant α the same five metres costs 2.50, 5.00, 10.00, 25.00 … as the drawing is refined (or 0, at α = 0). No constant can shrink exactly as fast as the cells multiply. Only the arc's own length does.

Here δa and δb are local: the length of the particular source segment and the particular target segment that step traverses — in segment mode, literally the arcs' own length. Three things follow, and they sharpen what α is. (i) If the sampling happens to be uniform, the length-weighted objective is not a new family at all: normalising by the advance (the gauge above) it is the (α, β) family, evaluated at a point that is computed, not tuned — α* = δb/(δa + δb), β* = δa/(δa + δb). But road vertices are not uniformly spaced — they crowd on curves and thin out on straights — so the correct discount is a local quantity that varies along the road: against a 5 m source segment it is 0.71 on a 12 m straight and 0.07 on a 0.4 m curve chord. No scalar α can be both. A single α is therefore not an approximation to the length-weighted objective but a compromise between its values, over-discounting the sparse stretches and under-discounting the dense ones. This is the sharpest form of the argument: the measure change does not give a better α, it makes α unnecessary, because it reads the two lengths off each step instead of asking the user to pick one number for the whole map. (The pipeline dulls the problem without curing it: match_dag densifies both sides at the same step_meters, forcing the spacings towards each other — which puts the ideal α near 0.5, not at the default 1 — but a 5 m step still yields spacings between 3.3 m and 7 m depending on edge length.) (ii) It does not subsume β: those weights put β* < 1, which discounts stalls, and the collapse of §9.5 survives them unchanged (5 stalls, 1 of 11 pairs correct, exactly as at α = β = 1). Length-weighting is a statement about the coverage side; the guard against N:1 compression remains an independent β ≥ 1. (iii) Its natural home is segment mode, and there it is a drop-in. The cost is a sum over steps, and every step traverses exactly one arc on each side that moves — so the length it should be charged is simply that arc's length, with no quadrature and nothing to attribute. Point mode obscures this: its states are vertices, and a vertex has no length, so one is forced to smear the adjacent arcs' lengths back onto their endpoints. Segment mode does not, because its states already are arcs, each carrying its own length (attached by line_digraph). The ledger's shape then survives intact and only its weights change:

entry pays ℓ(a)·E  (or β·ℓ(a)·E on a stall) ;   a coverage cell pays ℓ(v)·E   — the scalars 1, β, α replaced by the traversed arcs' own lengths.

(V4) then charges every source arc exactly once, so the consumed-once invariant carries over from cells to arcs, and no scalar α is left to choose — each state reads its own length. β stays, since the guard against collapse is independent of the measure.

Measured. A prototype of exactly this ledger (scripts/length_weighted_prototype.py) settles the question. Take one road — five 5 m source arcs, anchors ideally at 2.5, 7.5, 12.5, 17.5, 22.5 m — and draw the target more and more finely beside it. Under the shipped ledger the correspondence collapses as the target is drawn better: at a 1 m target sampling the anchors read 6.5, 7.5, 10.5, 15.5, 18.5; at 0.25 m they read 11.9, 12.1, 12.4, 12.6, 12.9; at 0.1 m the entire 25 m source has folded into half a metre of target, 12.2 … 12.7. Under the arc-length ledger the same inputs give 2.5, 7.5, 12.5, 17.5, 21.5 at 1 m and 2.5, 7.0, 11.9, 17.0, 21.8 at 0.1 m — the intended correspondence, unmoved across a 25× change in the target's sampling, and with no α anywhere in it. The artefact α is tuned against is not a small bias to be traded off; at fine samplings it is fatal, and the measure change removes it outright. That is the shape of the future work, and §9.5.2 is the measurement of what α costs as its stand-in.

8.4 Engines and outputs

match_dag(A, B, r, alpha, beta, mode, engine, bearing_weight, k_min) is the one-call pipeline — prepare → forward (the coupled pass) → extraction — and it returns the pair (M, committed): the matching relation, and, per source vertex, the cell its run begins at. The engine argument selects the extraction: "cell" (the default) is the cell-level join of §8.1; "join" is extract_join, the vertex-level junction join, kept only to cross-validate it; "all" runs both and returns the cheapest valid matching.

The vertex-level join minimises the same C(M) under the same judge, but it joins at vertex resolution: between junctions the stored back-pointer chains are deterministic, so each sink label induces exactly one label at every ancestor junction, the sink labels are the only free variables, and (V3) at a split becomes a join condition — all branches must induce the same split label. That resolution is also its ceiling. Its rows are vertex labels with stored histories, so intra-vertex alternatives — where a run starts, which cell of a run a child connects through — are frozen: it is exact over the stored-history family only, and it consumes D's values, whose exactness argument is tree-only (§7.1). Hence it is a cross-check, not the engine of record. The invariant C(cell) ≤ C(vertex join) whenever both succeed is pinned in the test suite — a violation would be an exactness bug by definition — and on the structured point-mode envelope the cell join returns 384/384 valid matchings against the vertex join's 379/384.

Downstream, the DuckDB pipeline (DuckDBMapMatcher.match_dag) runs segment mode and aggregates the arc-level matching back to input-edge level: dag_long, one row per (A-edge, B-edge) pair the matching connects; dag_summary, one row per A-edge with its destination set, mean drift and bearing difference, and a 1:1 / 1:N_ROUTE match type; and, with parts=True, dag_parts — the full per-edge decomposition, one row per part, a maximal run of consecutive A-arcs matched to the same B-edge (parts_from_matching is the standalone helper). The parts partition the A-edge (a_from_m/a_to_m contiguous and non-overlapping, Σ a_pct = 100) and carry the used span on the B side (b_from_m, b_to_m) together with its non-overlapping remainder (b_head_m, b_tail_m).

Route-level non-overlap falls out of the warping rules rather than a separate geometric pass. Because (V4) matches every A-arc, an A-edge that begins before, or ends after, what B actually covers cannot show a gap: its terminal arcs stall instead — several consecutive A-arcs riding one B-arc while B does not advance. The decomposition detects those terminal stall runs and emits the surplus arcs as their own rows with part_type = "head" / "tail", leaving the "match" parts — and their drift and bearing scores — free of the overhang. The begin/end non-overlap of a route is then read directly off both sides: A's from the head/tail rows, B's from the first part's b_head_m and the last part's b_tail_m.

9.4 The validator limit on a cyclic target

One structure defeats the validity predicate rather than the matcher. On a 2-cycle target p ⇄ q, (V1) is local and cannot orient a step, so on the smallest possible input — a 2-vertex source chain over p ⇄ q — every matching that separates the two source vertices, including the geometrically correct one, is flagged by the judge. This is a property of the predicate, not of the tables. The behaviour under it is the point: the cell-level join's root contraction leaves only the flagged row and it refuses loudly (ValueError); the vertex-level junction join returns the valid stall (both source vertices on one cell); match_dag(engine="all"), which takes the cheapest valid of the two, therefore still succeeds. All three behaviours are pinned by test_two_cycle_judge_prefers_valid. No engine, on any input in this evaluation, ever returned an invalid matching.

9.5 The weights α and β

The two weights have disjoint jobs and a fixed domain — α ∈ (0, 1], β ∈ [1, ∞), defaults α = β = 1. α discounts the emission charged on a 1:N coverage cell, so that riding a densely-sampled target run costs something closer to sampling-independent; β penalizes the emission charged on an N:1 stall, so the matcher spreads the source rather than collapsing it onto one target cell. Covering may be discounted; stacking is never free. Both act on the E-multiplier ledger — 1 on an advance or a free source entry, β on a stall, α on a coverage cell — which the forward relaxation and the cell-level join's backward pass apply identically, so the discount means the same thing in the table and in the extraction (Appendix D).

  • Monotone, no laundering. A worked example — β on a rigidly-shifted source. The abstract statement above ("the matcher spreads the source rather than collapsing it") has a sharp concrete form. Let A be a chain of 11 vertices 2 m apart, and let B be the same chain drawn 9 m further along the direction of travel and 0.5 m to the side. If that offset is a registration error — the two maps disagree about where one road lies — then the intended correspondence is ai ↔ bi, all 11 pairs. We stress at the outset that nothing in the geometry establishes this; §9.5.1 returns to it.

At β = 1 the matcher does not return that reading. A's leading vertices lie before B's first cell, and at β = 1 a stall and an advance carry the same multiplier on the emission of the cell being entered — so nothing offsets the nearer cell's smaller E, and the stall is strictly cheaper. (Concretely: a1 stalling on b0 costs 9.01 + 7.02 = 16.03, against 9.01 + 9.01 = 18.03 to advance to b1. Exact ties, when they occur, go to the advance.) The extraction therefore collapses the source's head onto b0 — 0,1,2,3,4,5 → b0, then 6→b1 … 10→b5 — an N:1 pile (Figure 1, top) that reproduces the plain nearest-cell reading of A exactly, and agrees with ai ↔ bi on 1 pair of 11. Its mean drift is 2.90 m, and that number is the trap: it is far lower than the 9.01 m of the shifted reading, because a nearest-cell match of an offset map always looks tighter than the offset itself. Low drift is not evidence of a correct correspondence — it is what a collapse looks like. (The depth of the pile is incidental: at β = 1 the six-deep and five-deep piles cost exactly the same, 31.92, because a5 sits midway between b0 and b1; the extraction's tie-break settles it. The collapse is the point, not its depth.)

β prices that stall, and the two readings scale differently in it. The 1:1 matching has no stall cell and no coverage cell, so its cost is not merely β-independent but weight-independent: C = 11·√(9² + 0.5²) = 99.15, flat forever. 9.5.1 What β does and does not do

It selects a prior; it does not recover a truth. The same input admits a second, equally coherent reading: that the coordinates are right and A's road simply starts 9 m before B's does — the different-extent case of §9.7. Under that prior the β = 1 collapse is not 1/11 wrong but 11/11 right, and the β = 8 answer is the wrong one (it consumes b6…b10, the 9 m of B beyond A's extent, which the β = 1 reading correctly leaves unmatched). The two are geometrically indistinguishable. β is the knob that chooses between them, and the drift column cannot adjudicate: a wrong rigid 1:1 across an offset also reports drift equal to the offset, "honestly". Nor, therefore, does raising β dominate β = 1 — it trades one class of error for the other. On a source that genuinely overruns its target (A: 6 vertices, x = 0…10; B: 13 cells, x = 6…30), β = 1 reads the head overhang correctly as a stall, and β = 5.5 — the value bolded above — destroys it, pairing A's head 1:1 onto cells it never reaches. Raise β when a systematic offset is known or suspected, not by default.

It cannot conjure cells that do not exist — but that is a counting condition, not a spatial one. Where the source has more vertices than the target has reachable cells, the pigeonhole forces a stall and no β removes it (11 source vertices over 5 reachable cells: 6 stalls at β = 1, 50 and 1000 alike). Spatial overrun alone does not, as the counter-case above shows. Two further preconditions bound the effect, and both are load-bearing: the search radius must admit the shifted pairs (at r = 15 a 15 m offset is unrecoverable at any β — its true pairs are never candidates), and a stall-free matching must exist in the candidate space at all.

β* falls as the offset grows. It is set by the shift measured in sampling steps, not in metres — and inversely: for this chain, β* ≈ 32.2 at a one-step offset, 8.6 at three steps, 5.1 at 4.5 steps, 2.5 at nine steps. The subtle offset, not the gross one, is the expensive case: it takes a large penalty to dislodge a collapse whose emissions are barely cheaper than the truth's. β* is therefore not a constant to hard-code, and the asymmetry α ≤ 1 ≤ β (§8.3) is a statement about the shape of the cost — covering a densely-sampled target may be discounted, stacking the source is never free — not a recipe for a value. 9.5.2 A worked example — α changes the relation

The mirror, and a sharper claim than "α encourages coverage". Take one road, matched against itself drawn five times more finely: the source A has 6 vertices every 5 m, the target B has 26 cells every 1 m, half a metre to the side. Because B is finer, 1:N coverage is forced — contiguity leaves each source vertex no choice but to ride a run of target cells, and it does so at every α. α does not decide whether to cover. It decides which cells each source vertex takes — and the two ends of its range are two different optimisation problems.

At α = 1 the objective is a sum over the whole relation. Every matched pair is charged its full emission, so each covered cell must earn its keep, and the matcher buys as little of B as it can: it shrinks the covered span to cells 4–21 of 0–25 and skews the runs to (1, 3, 5, 5, 3, 1). The consequence is not a cost artefact but a correspondence artefact — A0 stands at x = 0 and is anchored on the cell at x = 4. A four-metre registration error, produced by nothing but the density at which the other map was drawn.

At α → 0 the objective collapses to the entries. Only each run's first cell is charged, so what is being minimised is exactly Σa minv E(a, v): choose one 1:1 anchor cell per source vertex, and fill the gaps between the anchors at zero cost. Every vertex settles on the cell directly beneath it (0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25), the runs come out even (5, 5, 5, 5, 5, 1), and the cost is exactly 6 × 0.5 = 3.00. Between the two ends the relation slides continuously from one to the other (Figure 2). The cost falls too, of course — but the cost is not the point. The correspondence changes.

Why the domain is open at zero. As with β, there is no value that is right everywhere; the two weights simply fail in opposite directions.

9.6 Segment mode

Segment mode is not a heading term bolted onto point mode: the state is a segment pair. The identical algorithm — layering, forward table, V3 coupling, extraction, judge — runs on the line digraphs L(A), L(B), whose nodes are the directed arcs carrying midpoint and bearing, and the matching is emitted and validated on the arcs. It is shipped and it carries the same evidence as point mode: the cell-level join equals the full-space brute-force optimum on the line graphs, with the bearing term active (test_extract_cell_segment_equals_full_space_brute), and the two engines cross-validate across the segment scenarios — each returning engine is judged valid on L(A), L(B), and the C(cell) ≤ C(vertex join) invariant holds there too (test_cross_validation_segment). The lift also exercises reconvergence for free: a merge-and-split vertex of A becomes a bipartite arc cluster in L(A) — a reconvergent DAG — which is accepted natively, with both engines returning valid, agreeing matchings on it and the exactness claim carried, as always on reconvergent sources, by the cell engine.

The two failure modes this design exists to avoid are visible in the data even without a labelled gold standard. The first is direction: a two-way street is two opposing directed edges in the register (NVDB is_reverse twins) whose geometries are coincident, so any matcher that scores by undirected nearest distance is choosing between them by a coin flip — the nearest edge is as often the wrong-heading twin as the right one. 10 Discussion & Limitations

The evaluation supports a specific, bounded claim: for a subdivided directed source DAG — tree and reconvergent alike — the shipped matcher returns the exact minimum-cost matching over the full cell-level space, valid by (V1)–(V4) and judged as such on the returned relation. Exactness is anchored against full-space brute force (all entry-and-run combinations), including 195/195 exact on reconvergent sources — 75 jittered-diamond cases spanning three weight settings, plus 120 random subdivided reconvergent DAGs — and the structured 384-case envelope returns 384/384 valid matchings from the cell-level join. The vertex-level junction join (extract_join) is an independent cross-validation engine, not the engine of record: it joins at vertex resolution, so it is exact only over the stored-history family, scores 379/384 on the same envelope, and the invariant C(cell) ≤ C(vertex join) is pinned in the suite. match_dag(engine="all") runs both and returns the cheapest valid matching, with value == C(M) self-checked. The limitations below are equally important and are stated so the reader can calibrate what the method does and does not yet deliver.

10.1 The diamond needs the direction term, not more loop machinery

The residual failure in point mode is the reconvergent diamond under a large rigid lateral offset, where the two A-branches collapse onto the single nearer B-edge. It would be tempting to read this as an inference failure at an undirected cycle. It is not, and the exactness result settles the question: the cell-level join is the minimum-cost valid matching over the full space, verified to the digit against full-space brute force on exactly this family of jittered diamonds. If the collapse is what the engine returns, then the collapse is the minimum-drift optimum in point mode — the correct split simply costs more. No amount of additional loop machinery can recover the correspondence, because the correspondence is not the point-mode minimum.

The diagnosis is therefore a property of the objective, not of the inference. A middle-to-middle point distance is blind to a segment rotating about its own centre and, under a large lateral offset, to which of two nearby parallel edges is the corresponding one rather than the nearest one. The fix is the direction term, and it is shipped: segment mode (Section 7, Appendix E) runs the identical algorithm on the directed line graphs L(A), L(B), whose states are arc pairs carrying midpoint and bearing, so heading is pinned and parallel edges separate by orientation. Every state pays its emission — a stall costs βE, never zero — so the heading cannot be bypassed, and segment mode is validated with the bearing term active (cross-engine plus full-space brute force on the line graphs), λ ∈ [1, 5] being the recommended regime.

A note on what is reported. The reported C(M) is the realized cost of the returned relation: the root join's value equals C(M) exactly by the E-multiplier ledger (each cell's emission charged once — 1 on an advance or a free source entry, β on a stall, α on a coverage cell) and the equality is self-checked. The forward table's D values are not a reported cost and are never used as one: they carry the shared-cone 1/outdeg fractions and serve the extraction only as pruning (a forbidden or D = ∞ cell can appear in no row). The older two-table traceback's D[j][v] + B[j][v] − E(j,v) seed is unsound at a merge — its exact-optimum claim was disproven — and extract_two_table survives only as a diagnostic.

10.2 Cross-validation is the test harness, and it found real defects

Two independent extractions over one shared objective and one shared judge give a harness with teeth: whenever the cell-level join and the vertex-level junction join disagree, one of them is wrong, and the disagreement localizes the defect. Two standing results came out of that workflow.

  1. The two-table traceback's exactness claim was disproven. Seeding a traceback at the joint D+B−E argmin is unsound at a merge, where the two tables' shared-cone fractions do not cancel. It was demoted from an extraction to the extract_two_table diagnostic; the cell-level join replaced it.
  2. The vertex join's limit was characterized, not papered over. Its rows are vertex labels with stored histories, so intra-vertex alternatives — where a run starts, which cell of a run a child connects through — are frozen. It therefore diverges from the cell engine exactly in dense-target coverage regimes, at any weights, and a pinned divergence case shows the cell join strictly beating it and equalling the full-space optimum (the optimal coverage run is one the forward pointers never stored). This is a resolution limit of the vertex join, not a candidate-gating artefact: it is why the vertex join is a cross-check and the cell join is the extraction.

Earlier iterations of this work resolved reconvergence with separate conditioning solvers over a message-passing forest base (in the spirit of belief propagation, Pearl, 1988, and cutset conditioning, Dechter, 1990); they are design history, superseded by the current design — the (V3) forbid-and-rebuild coupling, which enforces split agreement during the build on cells rather than after the fact, plus the cell-level join, which is exact on reconvergent sources without any conditioning layer at all.

10.3 Two limits: cyclic sources, and the validator on a cyclic target

These are distinct and worth keeping distinct.

  • Cyclic sources are unsupported. The source must be acyclic: forks, merges, and reconvergences (diamonds, divided roads) are all accepted by default, but a directed cycle in A raises NotADAG. Only the source is so constrained — the target B may cycle freely (roundabouts, grids).
  • The validator's limit on a cyclic target. On a 2-cycle p ⇄ q in B, the local (V1) predicate cannot orient a step: on the smallest such case — a 2-vertex chain over p ⇄ q — the geometrically correct matching, and every separating alternative in both directions, is flagged. This is a property of the predicate, not of the matcher. The behaviour is explicit rather than silent: the cell join refuses loudly (its root contraction leaves only the flagged row), the vertex join returns the valid stall, and engine="all" therefore still succeeds — pinned by test_two_cycle_judge_prefers_valid.

Feasibility is likewise loud, never silent: candidates are gated by a radius r = match_radius_m, and an emptied split, an unreachable vertex, a vertex with no surviving cell, or an all-invalid root join raises ValueError — located, naming the offending split, vertex, or component wherever the failure has one — telling you to increase match_radius_m.

10.4 Scope and shipped-vs-reference status

Three scope caveats bound the claims:

  • The vertex-level join is cross-validation only. extract_join is kept to validate and cross-validate the extraction, never as the engine of record: it is exact over the stored-history family only, it consumes D values whose exactness argument is tree-only, and on reconvergent sources it is a judged cross-check. extract_cell is the engine of record.
  • Synthetic-only numbers. The reported figures come from the structured envelope and the brute-force anchors, in point and segment mode (bearing active). They validate correctness and exactness of the algorithm, not accuracy on real OSM↔NVDB data.
  • Count-weighted objective. C(M) sums per-cell emissions and is therefore density-dependent through step_meters; a length-weighted, density-independent objective is future work.

11 Conclusion & Future Work

We have presented a projection-based, directed-graph generalization of dynamic time warping for road-network conflation. Graph-DTW aligns a single directed A-edge to a local directed target graph GB that may contain cycles, replacing DTW's scalar predecessor with a Dijkstra over GB's arcs on the horizontal move; DAG-DTW lifts the source from a path to a connected acyclic subgraph GA — forks, merges, and reconvergences included — and solves a whole junction neighbourhood at once, so that A-edges meeting at a junction agree on where that junction lands in B. That agreement is not repaired afterwards: it is enforced during the build by the (V3) forbid-and-rebuild coupling, which acts on cells, never on vertices, and closes the split-optimism of the forward sum. The answer is then read off by the cell-level join — a backward sweep that rebuilds per-vertex tables from the emissions alone, contracted per interface, with the relation travelling in the row, so selection is simply the cheapest row that the judge (check_rules, (V1)–(V4)) accepts. The 1/outdeg split factor makes the forward sum exact on a tree and the forward table serves the extraction only as pruning, so the extraction's exactness does not inherit the tree restriction: it is exact over the full cell-level space, including reconvergent sources — 195/195 against full-space brute force there, 384/384 valid on the structured envelope against the vertex join's 379/384, with C(cell) ≤ C(vertex join) pinned and 180 tests green. The library ships both modes (point, and segment on the line graphs), a one-call match_dag, and an edge-level decomposition (dag_parts) that splits each source edge into match / head / tail parts, detecting route begin/end non-overlap from terminal stall runs.

The evaluation is deliberately honest about where the objective, rather than the inference, sets the boundary: in point mode a reconvergent diamond under a large lateral offset genuinely minimizes drift by collapsing onto the nearer B-edge, and the exact engine confirms that this is the true point-mode optimum. The answer is the direction term, which segment mode carries by construction — the middle-to-middle segment emission with the bearing term,

(14) E(i,e) = &lVert;mid(ai,ai+1) − mid(u,v)&rVert; + λ dcirc(θ(si), θ(e)),

evaluated on states that are segment pairs (the nodes of L(A) and L(B)) rather than vertex pairs dressed with a heading. Because the bearing term pins heading and separates geometrically-close parallel edges by orientation, it is the principled fix for the nearest-vs-corresponding collapse — not more loop machinery — and the working regime λ ∈ [1, 5] carries over from graph-DTW.

Future work runs in four directions. A length-weighted, sampling-density-independent objective would remove the residual dependence of C(M) on step_meters. A symmetric, network-wide B → A reconciliation pass over the per-edge matcher would lift local optima to global consistency. Cyclic sources are presently rejected (NotADAG); the pragmatic path is a fallback to per-edge graph-DTW, which already handles a cyclic target, on local components whose source side is not acyclic, with exactness on cyclic sources kept as a gated research track rather than a promise. And validation on real OSM↔NVDB corridors — the setting the method was designed for — remains the essential empirical next step beyond the synthetic correctness suite reported here.

Appendix A — DP recurrences

This appendix states the forward table of DAG-DTW in full — weights, coupling and all — derives the split factor and the conserved-flow reading that motivates it, mirrors the table backward, and works the Y-split example by hand. Throughout, GA is the source local DAG and GB the target local directed graph (B-vertices, possibly cyclic). Each pair (a,v) of an A-vertex with one of its radius-gated candidates is a cell; a cell holds the emission E(a,v) of Appendix E (in point mode E(a,v) = ‖a−v‖), the forward cost D, its back-pointer bpD, and a forbidden flag. Apred(a) and Bpred(v) denote graph-predecessors in GA and GB, and outdeg(a) the out-degree of a in GA.

A.1 The forward table D

D[a][v] is the minimum cost of matching a's upstream cone — every A-vertex topologically above a covered — with a pinned at B-vertex v. For each predecessor p ∈ Apred(a) write its two ways into v (every look-up intersected with the gated candidate sets; a cell pruned by r is ∞):

stepp = minx∈Bpred(v) D[p][x]  (advance),     stallp = D[p][v]  (p already on v)

The recurrence is a three-way min over the three legal moves, each charging the emission at its own weight — a full E on a 1:1 advance, βE on an N:1 stall, αE on a 1:N coverage step:

(15) D[a][v] = min {
  E(a,v) + Σp steppoutdeg(p)  (D)  every branch advances — full E
  β E(a,v) + minq [ stallqoutdeg(q) + Σp≠q min(stallp, stepp)outdeg(p) ]  (V)  ≥1 branch stalls — N:1, βE
  α E(a,v) + minv′∈Bpred(v) D[a][v′]  (H)  1:N coverage along B, αE
}

with p, q ranging over Apred(a). The weights have domain α ∈ (0,1] (coverage discount, Appendix D) and β ∈ [1,∞) (stall penalty), defaults α = β = 1, at which (15) reproduces the unweighted cost bit-for-bit; the older unweighted statement D = E + min((H), (A)) — a single advance branch (A) = Σp minv′∈Bpred(v)∪{v} D[p][v′] / outdeg(p) — is exactly that special case: at α = β = 1 the union of (D) and (V) is precisely (A), since min(stallp, stepp) is the v′∈Bpred(v)∪{v} minimum. It is unsafe as a general definition because that same min merges stall and advance at equal weight, which the (V) branch exists to keep apart. The inner minq is what makes that separation exact: it forces one genuine stall, so β is never charged on a pure advance.

The (D)/(V) branches encode two structural facts:

  1. Sum over Apred(a) (coverage, not routing). At a merge, the costs of all incoming A-edges are added. D[a][v] is not "the cheapest path through GA to a"; it is "the cheapest way to align everything above a, with a pinned to v." A min here would silently discard an incoming branch. The rule is sum over branches, min over B-positions — and the sum is finite only if every branch reaches v, which folds (V2) into the cost.
  2. The three DTW moves are the chain special case. When a has a single predecessor a′ with outdeg(a′) = 1, both sums collapse to one term and (15) reads
(16) D[a][v] = min( αE + D[a][v′] horizontal , βE + D[a′][v] vertical , E + D[a′][v′] diagonal ),   E = E(a,v),   v′∈Bpred(v)

i.e. the graph-DTW recurrence (vertical = A advances on the same B-vertex — a stall; horizontal = B advances on the same A-vertex — coverage; diagonal = both), with the scalar DTW predecessor j−1 replaced by "any B-predecessor of v" (Sakoe & Chiba, 1978; Berndt & Clifford, 1994). DAG-DTW is thus a strict superset of graph-DTW: a single-path GA is graph-DTW.

Boundary conditions arise with no special-casing.

  • Source A-vertex (Apred(a) = ∅): the (D) sum is empty = 0 and (V) has no branch to stall, so the base is D[a][v] = E(a,v) — free entry at every candidate B-vertex.
  • Sink A-vertex: no outgoing edge consumes D[a][·]; the sweep leaves it free — free exit.

The coverage move is a within-row fixed point. Unlike (D)/(V), which read only predecessor rows, (H) self-references D[a][·] within the same A-vertex, and GB carries no order of its own — it may contain directed cycles (roundabouts, grids), so there is no topological sweep of B to lean on. The row is therefore relaxed until nothing changes: the (D)/(V) base is computed first, cell by cell, and then, repeatedly over every B-arc v→w inside the row,

(17) D[a][w] ← min( D[a][w], D[a][v] + α E(a,w) ),    bpD[a][w] ← [(a,v)] on a strict decrease

swept until a full pass changes nothing. The cost is lowered and the back-pointer repointed in the same step, so the pointer always names the cell that produced the value. Since αE ≥ 0 this is a monotone descent to the unique least fixed point — correct even on a cyclic B, where any single pass would leave cells un-relaxed. One A-vertex may therefore ride a whole run of B-vertices when B is denser than A. Note the advance and stall branches are computed in the base, not recovered by relaxation: relaxing into v from a stall-seeded neighbour v′ would pay E(a,v′) + αE(a,v), which is not the advance cost. (Implemented as _fill_row, driven by _pass / forward in dag_dtw.py.)

Two housekeeping rules complete the row. Every argmin above breaks exact-cost ties by one fixed total order on B's vertices (_b_order, sorted by id), identically in every pass — this changes only which of two equal cells is stored, leaving costs and optimality untouched, and makes the tables invariant to B's insertion order. And every place the recurrence links to a neighbour cell — advance source, stall source, same-row coverage source — skips a cell whose forbidden flag is set; the flagged cell's own value is still computed, but no back-pointer may ever target it.

Fill order and the V3 coupling. The rows are filled in longest-path layer order (layer_order): a source gets L = 0, every other vertex L(v) = maxp∈Apred(v) L(p) + 1, vertices sorted by L ascending (ties by id). A plain topological sort does not suffice — it can slip a successor of one split-child ahead of that child's sibling — whereas on a subdivided source, layering puts a split's children in one layer, complete as a group before anything downstream of them is filled. (The pass raises ValueError if a split's children span layers: the source was not subdivided.)

That grouping is what the coupling needs. A row is not final once filled: filled independently, two children of a split can link back to different cells of the split — a (V3) break the forward sum cannot see, since a sum of per-branch minima can place one vertex in two places at once. The shipped forward pass therefore interleaves the V3 coupling (forbid-and-rebuild, Section 5) after every row: as each child of a split completes — including the first — every cell of the split it does not link to is marked forbidden, dead as a pointer target for all siblings past and future; every already-built sibling that leaned on a newly-forbidden exit has its entire row rebuilt under the current flags (the within-row (H) chains make a single-cell patch impossible), and the step iterates. The forbidden set only grows, so this is a monotone descent to a fixed point in at most one round per candidate cell of the split. The coupling acts on cells, never on vertices — identical in both modes, indifferent to how many parents a child has. At the fixed point every surviving exit of every split is linked by all its children (invariant: check_split_exits); several survivors are legitimate — the extraction chooses among them — and an emptied split raises the feasibility ValueError (increase match_radius_m).

A.2 The split factor and the conserved-flow reading

The factor 1/outdeg(p) — computed as max(1, |Asucc(p)|), so chain and merge vertices divide by 1 and only splits divide — is what makes the forward sum well-defined on a branching source. Read it as a cost-flow conservation law: each A-vertex divides its accumulated cost equally among its outgoing A-edges, so that — summed across all sinks — every vertex's emission E is counted exactly once. The reading this motivates is the pencil-and-paper quantity

(18) Σsinks t minv D[t][v]

Identity (tree case). If GA is a tree/polytree and α = β = 1, (18) equals Σa E(a,·) over the vertices, each emission counted once.

Argument. On a tree, orient each A-vertex a toward the sinks it feeds. Along the advance branch, a's emission is multiplied on the way to each descendant sink by the product of 1/outdeg over the splits on that path. The number of sink-paths leaving a equals ∏ outdeg over exactly those same splits, and each path carries an equal share; the shares therefore sum to 1. Summing minv D[t][v] over the sinks re-assembles each emission with total weight 1. ∎

Two caveats, both load-bearing. First, the identity is tree-only: on a reconvergent DAG (a diamond) the branches meeting at a merge can share an ancestor, and the forward sum double-counts it, so no exactness is claimed for D's values on reconvergent sources. Second — and this is why the first caveat costs nothing — (18) is never computed by the engine. It is the argument for the split factor, not a diagnostic the pipeline reports. The extraction (Section 8) is the cell-level join (extract_cell), which builds everything from the emissions alone; the stored D/bpD propagation is never read for its values — the forward table serves the extraction only as pruning (a forbidden or D = ∞ cell can appear in no row) and, through the coupling, as the enforcer of (V3). The winning row carries the matching relation with it: no traceback, no reconstruction.

The reported quality metric is therefore the cost of the returned relation M ⊆ V(A)×V(B),

(19) C(M) = Σ(a,v)∈M w(a,v) E(a,v),    w = 1 (advance) · α (coverage) · β (stall)

where M(a) = {v : (a,v) ∈ M} is a run of target cells, not a single label — the output is a relation, not a function φ. The headline geometric figure stays the raw drift ΣE over M's cells, unweighted.

A.3 The backward table B

B[a][v] is the mirror of D: the cost of matching a's downstream cone with a pinned at v. It is produced by the same row-filling body with both graphs reversed — GA swept in reverse topological order and read through Asucc, every GB arc flipped — and the split factor replaced by the symmetric 1/indeg, with the same α, β and the same emission:

(20) B[a][v] = min { E + Σs stepsindeg(s) ,   βE + minq [ stallqindeg(q) + Σs≠q min(stalls, steps)indeg(s) ] ,   αE + minv′∈Bsucc(v) B[a][v′] }

with s, q ranging over Asucc(a), steps = minx∈Bsucc(v) B[s][x] and stalls = B[s][v], so conserved flow holds in the reverse direction too.

What the table is for. backward is a diagnostic, not a stage of matching: it drives no extraction. It feeds the cross-table checks (check_reciprocity — every source edge the forward pointers thread, the backward pointers must thread back identically on the committed matching; check_backward_v2) and the legacy two-table agreement extraction (extract_two_table), retained as a diagnostic. D and B jointly powered that earlier two-table procedure — an earlier iteration, superseded by the cell-level join once its D+B−E seed was shown to be unsound at a merge (Appendix B, design history). One ordering constraint survives: because the backward pass respects the forbidden flags, it must run after the coupled forward pass, so that its pointers never target dead cells.

A.4 Worked Y-split example

Take the clean Y-split: A-vertices a0→a1, then a1→a2 and a1→a3, so a0 is the source, a1 the split (outdeg(a1) = 2), a2,a3 the sinks. Suppose every A-vertex drifts a constant δ = 0.2 onto its intended B-twin, take α = β = 1, and (for a clean hand-check) assume each vertex is entered by the diagonal advance (D) onto its own twin — no stall, no coverage — so each D picks up one E = 0.2 plus its split-scaled predecessor:

(21) D[a0] = 0.2   (source: free entry, E only)
D[a1] = 0.2 + 11·D[a0] = 0.2 + 0.2 = 0.4   (outdeg(a0) = 1)
D[a2] = 0.2 + 12·D[a1] = 0.2 + ½(0.4) = 0.4   (split share 1/outdeg(a1) = ½)
D[a3] = 0.2 + 12·D[a1] = 0.4

The conserved-flow reading (18) over the two sinks is

(22) minv D[a2][v] + minv D[a3][v] = 0.4 + 0.4 = 0.8 = 0.2·4

which is exactly δ times the four A-vertices — each emission counted once. A naive sum without the split factor would give D[a2] = D[a3] = 0.2 + 0.4 = 0.6 and a total of 1.2, double-counting a0 and a1. That is the whole moral of the factor. The coincidence with the realized C(M) = 0.8 of (19) is the tree-case identity of §A.2 and nothing more: the source is a tree, α = β = 1, and every emission is charged once on both readings. Add a reconvergence and the two part company — which is precisely why the shipped extraction never reads D's values at all.

Appendix B — The extraction & exactness

The forward table of Appendix A is a table, not a matching. This appendix explains why the matching cannot be read off per-junction, states the shipped extraction — the cell-level join (extract_cell, dag_dtw.py:705) — records the earlier iterations it superseded, and gives its exactness result.

B.1 Why per-junction argmin is inconsistent

Consider two routes s1→t1 and s2→t2 that pass through a common junction j∈GA. Optimised independently, route 1 prefers j at one B-vertex and route 2 at a different one — but j occupies only one position. A greedy per-sink backtrack that traces each route back from its own cheapest sink will have the two traces collide at j with incompatible demands; forcing a single position satisfies one route and forces the other to take a backward step — a non-forward B-transition on a GA arc, i.e. a route that must run against B's digitisation to reach its next fixed cell, exactly what (V1) forbids. Crucially this happens even when every A-edge is mapped to the correct B-edge: the failure is in the shared position of the junction, not the edge assignment. Summing such independent minima can put one vertex in two places at once — a phantom no matching realizes. One therefore cannot assemble the whole matching from each-sink's-own-best pieces.

The shipped design draws two consequences. (i) The split-optimism is closed during the build, by the V3 coupling (forbid-and-rebuild, Appendix A), which acts on cells, never on vertices: a cell of a split that is not linked by every child is marked forbidden, every sibling row that linked it is rebuilt, and the forbidden set grows to a fixed point, after which every surviving exit of every split is linked by all its children. (ii) The matching is then extracted by a join over tables built backward from the sinks — never by a per-sink backtrack, and never by a per-junction argmin.

B.2 The shipped extraction: the cell-level join

The extraction lives on one object, the cell-move graph: its nodes are the cells (a,v) of the gated candidate rows, its edges the three legal moves — cover (a,v)→(a,w), w∈Bsucc(v) (a extends its run one B-arc); stall (p,v)→(c,v) (child enters on its parent's cell, N:1); advance (p,v)→(c,w), w∈Bsucc(v) (child enters one B-arc ahead, 1:1). A matching is an embedding of GA into this graph: each A-vertex maps to a cover path — its run M(a) — and each A-edge is realized by exactly one stall/advance edge from the parent's run end to the child's run entry. (V1)–(V4) say precisely that such an embedding exists and covers every vertex. The extraction is therefore a minimum-cost embedding, not a shortest path: a path cannot fork at a split, nor make two arms agree at a merge.

It is solved by a dynamic program in one reverse-topological sweep of GA, sinks first, every child's table complete before its parent's. Each A-vertex X carries a table of rows (entry, value, pending, cells): the cell of X its parent will connect to; the cost of everything at and below X; the merge entries still unpaid; and the embedding decided so far — a map from each decided A-vertex to its run. M travels with the row: the winning row's cells map is the relation, so there is no traceback and no gap-fill. Costs follow one E-multiplier ledger — every cell's emission is charged exactly once, weighted by the move that enters it: 1 on a source entry or an advance, β on a stall, α on a cover cell. For a run R = e→…→u of X with children c∈Asucc(X) entering at their own cells ce,

(23) value(X,e,R) = Σv∈R∖{e} αE(X,v) + Σc∈Asucc(X) [ w(c)·E(c,ce) + value(c,ce,·) ],   w(c) = β if ce = u (stall), 1 if ce ∈ Bsucc(u) (advance)

Two deferrals make the ledger consumed-once. X's own entry-E is not in (23) — whether it costs 1 or β is the parent's choice of edge, so the parent pays it on connection (a source pays its own, in full: free entry). And a merge serving several parent arms is absorbed by the first arm to reach it — the others receive only its interface — with its entry-E parked as a pending key that every arm must present with the same cell, settled once when the arms meet (β if any arm stalled onto it). This is why no 1/indeg and no split factor appears anywhere in the extraction, and why it carries no tree dependence: on a reconvergent source the arms' pendings must simply agree at their shared ancestor's join. Rows are then contracted per (entry, pending) interface — two rows with the same interface are interchangeable to everything upstream, so only the cheaper survives. That contraction is the polynomial bound: it is what keeps the join a join rather than a cross-product of subtree choices.

Selection is not a search. The sweep ends with one table per source; the root join folds them into whole-graph rows (pendings must agree on every merge's entry cell), pays every deferred merge entry once, and the joined value then equals C(M) exactly — self-checked. Rows are tried cheapest-first, and the first whose relation passes check_rules — the judge, (V1)–(V4) on the final relation — is returned, with committed[a] = run[0]. The judge's word is final: the extraction never returns an invalid matching; if no row survives, a located ValueError is raised (increase match_radius_m).

Everything above is built from the emissions E alone. The forward table serves the extraction only as pruning — a forbidden cell (the B.1 coupling) or a D = ∞ cell can appear in no row, and a reverse pre-pass from all sink cells over the cell-move graph (_cell_reachable) removes every cell that reaches no sink. Its stored back-pointers bpD are never consulted: they are one chosen history per cell — the argmin froze the very choices the extraction is optimising — and the optimal coverage run is sometimes one the pointers never stored.

B.3 Earlier iterations

Two designs preceded the cell-level join, and are recorded here because the intuition survives them. The first scored each junction once, for everyone passing through it, from a forward table D and a backward table B: φ(j) = argminv ( D[j][v] + B[j][v] − E(j,v) ), a min-sum message combination (Pearl, 1988) with the −E removing the double count. The second added a reachability-guarded backtrack that swept vertices in reverse-topological order and committed labels subject to a GB reachability test. Neither is the shipped procedure. The backward table is now a diagnostic mirror that fills only when backward() is explicitly run, and the D+B−E combination survives only as the seed of two-table agreement (extract_two_table, dag_dtw.py:379 — "a PREVIOUS extraction, kept only for the cross-table diagnostics"), whose "exact optimum" claim was disproven: the seed is unsound at a merge. No φ-labelling resolver exists in the shipped code, and there is no continuous re-match stage — the output is the discrete relation M plus committed[a] = run[0]; continuous geometry (spans along each edge, head/tail non-overlap) appears only downstream, in the dag_parts edge-level decomposition (parts_from_matching).

B.4 Exactness

The tree kernel. On a tree, a merge's incoming branches live in disjoint upstream subtrees — if they shared an ancestor, that ancestor would fork and rejoin, an undirected cycle, which a tree forbids. So the branches can be optimised independently and their costs added at the merge: one number per cell suffices, and the forward sum at a merge is exact. This is the whole content of the classical min-sum argument, and it is also the ceiling of the vertex-level machinery: the vertex-level junction join (extract_join), which the repository keeps as the cross-validation engine, consumes D's values and joins at vertex resolution, so its exactness is confined to the stored-history family, and on a reconvergent source — where two branches can share an ancestor and the forward sum double-counts it — it is a judged cross-check, not a proof.

The shipped claim. The cell-level join never reads D's values: it rebuilds every cost from E under the ledger of B.2, at cell granularity, over the full space of entries and coverage runs. Its exactness is therefore not tree-bound. Claim. extract_cell returns a matching of minimum C(M) over all valid warpings inside the candidate gate — on trees and on reconvergent DAGs. The root join's value equals C(M) by the ledger (each cell's E charged exactly once, by the move entering it), and the contraction discards only rows dominated at an identical interface, so no optimum is lost. The claim is stated over the gated, coupled space: cells the (V3) coupling marks forbidden are never re-examined by the extraction, and on reconvergent sources that pruning is verified empirically rather than proved — the one gap between the claim and a proof. Verified to the digit against full-space brute force — every entry-and-run combination — on tiny dense-target cases in point and segment mode, on random mini polytrees, and on reconvergent DAG sources: 195/195 exact (75 jittered-diamond runs across 3 weight settings + 120 random subdivided reconvergent DAGs), all valid. ∎

The structured envelope (scripts/test_dag_point.py: chain/ysplit/merge/deep × 4 sampling-density pairs × 4 rigid shifts × 2 noise levels × 3 (α,β) settings = 384 cases, both engines) gives cell join 384/384 valid, vertex-level junction join 379/384; the invariant C(cell) ≤ C(vertex join) is pinned in the suite — a violation would be an exactness bug by definition. Reconvergence is thus not an exception regime and needs no flag: the diamond is a legal, natively handled input, and the only source structure the matcher refuses is a directed cycle (NotADAG). The conditioning solvers of Appendix C are the earlier iterations this exactness result retired. The one remaining limit is on the target side and belongs to the validator, not the matcher: on a 2-cycle in B the local (V1) predicate cannot orient a step, so the cell join refuses loudly while the vertex join returns the valid stall — engine="all", which returns the cheapest valid of the two, still succeeds.

Appendix C — Reconvergence conditioning (an earlier iteration)

This appendix is design history. It records an earlier iteration in which a pair of exact conditioning solvers — belief propagation on a forest, wrapped in a vertex cut — was built (in a prototype module since removed from the tree, together with its own tests and validation harness) to close the one structure the then-current forward–backward extraction could not resolve: a reconvergent diamond. The shipped engine answers that structure differently, and without conditioning: the forward table carries the V3 coupling (forbid-and-rebuild, Appendix A), and the extraction is the cell-level join (extract_cell), which never consumes D's values at all — so the shared-ancestor double-count that motivated conditioning never enters. It is exact over the full space on reconvergent DAG sources: 195/195 against full-space brute force (75 jittered diamonds × 3 weight settings + 120 random subdivided reconvergent DAGs; tests/test_dag_dtw.py). The solvers below were retired, not held in reserve. We keep the appendix because its diagnosis, its two bugs, and its closing finding (§C.6) all survived the redesign and shaped it.

C.1 The structural problem

An undirected cycle in a DAG occurs exactly where two directed paths from a common ancestor re-meet: a diamond with split j1→{up,down}→ merge j2. On such a cycle the two branches share both endpoints, so they are no longer independent and their costs may not simply be added. The forward–backward estimator of the day let each side choose its own label where the sides re-meet — B[j1][v] summed the branches as if each could independently pick j2 — so the two disagreed and a backward step appeared. The reachability guard of Appendix B could not repair this, because the loop constrains j1 and j2 simultaneously in both directions; min-sum message passing is exact only when the graph has no undirected cycle (Pearl, 1988).

Two facts followed, measured on a perturbed diamond, and both still worth stating to avoid over-claiming: D+B−E at the split read 112.8 while Σsinks min D read 96.9 — they agree exactly on every tree but not here — and neither equalled the realized Ctotal (on the diamond the DP's own table value read 96.9 against a realized 183.6 under a joint-consistent shift). So a table value is a DP diagnostic, not a bound — and the D+B−E seed is unsound at a merge, which is why the two-table agreement it drives survives only as a diagnostic (extract_two_table). The shipped cell-level join sidesteps the whole difficulty structurally rather than by cutting the loop: it rebuilds every cost from the emissions upstream, so no shared ancestor is ever summed twice and no branch ever pre-commits the merge's label.

C.2 The exact min-sum belief-propagation forest solver

Both conditioning solvers rested on one exact base — call it the forest solver: min-sum (max-product) belief propagation on a polytree (Pearl, 1988). The model was a pairwise MRF over A-vertex labels:

  • Unary potential ψa(v) = E(a,v) plus any folded constraints from pinned-boundary reachability (an A-vertex adjacent to a pinned junction inherits a hard +∞ where it cannot forward-reach that pin).
  • Pairwise potential on each GA arc a→a′: ψa,a′(v,v′) = 0 if v′∈ReachGB(v) (the directed reachability constraint φ(head)∈Reach(φ(tail))) and +∞ otherwise.

On any tree this returns the exact minimum, and — the correctness-critical detail — it returned the BP value itself (+∞ for an infeasible pinning), never a drift re-summed off an argmin fallback (see §C.5). Conditioning is exact only because this base is exact: the reachability-guarded backtrack of Appendix B was a heuristic and could not serve as the base, a lesson §C.5 paid for.

C.3 Solver 1 — recursive minimum-vertex-cut

The recursive solver: if the free part of GA was already a forest, it ran BP and returned. Otherwise it picked a small vertex cut S (in practice the min-feedback-vertex-set vertices, usually a single junction), enumerated its K-nearest B-candidates, pinned S to each combination, and recursed into the now-independent components GA−S. Complexity (#cand)w where w is the largest single cut (usually 1) — linear in the number of loops: a chain of k diamonds costs ∼ k·#cand.

C.4 Solver 2 — one-shot minimum feedback-vertex-set

The one-shot solver: compute a minimum FVS F — the smallest vertex set whose removal leaves a forest — enumerate all joint label combinations of F at once, and solve the forest GA−F with BP for each. Complexity (#cand)|F| — exponential in the number of loops. FVS describes when the problem is loop-free (F = ∅ ⇔ tree) but is a poor way to label at scale; it earned its keep only as the independent cross-check on the recursive solver. Finding a minimum cut / FVS is NP-hard in general (Karp, 1972), but local junction graphs are tiny and a greedy separator sufficed.

Note what the equivalence chain does not say today. F = ∅ ⇔ tree was, at the time, also the boundary of exactness, because the extraction of the day was exact only on trees. It is no longer: only the vertex-level junction join (extract_join) carries a tree-scoped exactness argument, and it is kept purely as the cross-validation engine — the engine of record, the cell-level join, is exact on reconvergent DAG sources too. Loop-freeness stopped being the frontier the moment the extraction stopped reading D.

C.5 Why the two had to agree, and the bugs the agreement caught

Both solvers minimised the same realized cost Σa E(a,φ(a)) with the same exact forest base, differing only in how they cut loops. Therefore, with a full candidate set, on any DAG they had to return equal-cost labellings. That mutual invariant was itself the test harness: any disagreement localised a bug. It held wherever both solvers ran — on the diamond and on the double diamond (|F| = 2, the case that actually separates the two methods: both at 28.93), against an independent brute-force optimum on tiny cases, and across a 5×3×3 perturbation grid, 225/225 configs agreeing. Those figures are historical: they came from a harness that was deleted with the module it validated, and nothing in the current tree reproduces them. (The standing validation of the shipped engine is a different harness and a different claim: the cell-level join exact 195/195 against full-space brute force on reconvergent DAG sources, and 384/384 valid on the structured point-mode envelope, scripts/test_dag_point.py.)

The invariant caught two genuine correctness bugs (commit d289ffe), both triggered by anti-parallel B geometry (a B-edge digitised against A's flow — normal in real conflation):

  1. Infeasibility mis-scored as cheap-and-valid. The forest solver had re-summed drift over an argmin=0 fallback of an all-∞ belief, so an infeasible pinned boundary (an A-arc forced backward in B) was scored as a cheap valid labelling, undercutting the true optimum. Fix: return the BP value itself as the cost, propagating +∞ instead of laundering it.
  2. Inter-F backward arcs invisible. The forest solver folded only pinned-to-free constraints, so arcs internal to the pinned set running backward were never checked. Fix: a feasibility predicate on the pinning itself, rejecting any pinning whose internal arcs ran backward in B.

An earlier symptom of the same class: the double diamond first disagreed (29.09 vs 29.33) when the forest base was the heuristic reachability-guarded backtrack recorded in Appendix B; swapping in exact min-sum BP made both agree at 28.93 — below either heuristic value. The lesson the paper carries forward: the labels are the hard part; conditioning buys exactness only on top of an exact base. The shipped engine takes the same lesson one step further — it makes the base exact and then needs no conditioning at all. Its cross-check is not a second cut solver but the vertex-level junction join, with the invariant C(cell) ≤ C(vertex join) pinned in the suite and match_dag(engine="all") returning the cheapest valid of the two under the judge, check_rules.

C.6 The honest limit — the diamond needs the direction term, not a cut

The decisive finding — the one that ended the conditioning line — is that the diamond's failure is not a loop-conditioning failure. Pin the split j1 to the exact B-split and solve the branches in point mode: both A-branches still collapse onto the nearer B-edge, because that genuinely costs less under a pure-distance emission — the correct split never appears at any cut label. The exact solvers only confirmed that the collapse is the true minimum-drift optimum in point mode. The error is therefore objective-level, not loop-level: conditioning reduces but cannot remove the nearest-vs-corresponding error, and the real fix is the direction term — the segment-mode bearing emission of Appendix E, which the shipped matcher runs on the line digraphs.

So the shipped pipeline, in both point and segment mode, is the V3-coupled forward pass plus the cell-level join, judged by check_rules; the vertex-level junction join cross-validates it and the two-table agreement remains a diagnostic. The conditioning solvers were retired — not shelved alongside — once the cell-level join delivered exactness on reconvergent sources with no cut at all. Which is this appendix's closing lesson, and the reason it is worth keeping: the labels were never the hard part the cut could fix.

Appendix D — Coverage weight α

When B is sampled more densely than A, one A-vertex must cover a run of B-vertices via repeated horizontal (H) moves. The plain (H) move re-pays the emission E(a,·) at every covered B-vertex, so a 1:N match costs linearly in B's sampling density — an arbitrary artefact of Δ. The coverage weight α discounts it: it is the alpha argument of forward(A, B, alpha, beta) (dag_dtw.py:295) and of match_dag (dag_dtw.py:1131), with domain α ∈ (0, 1] and default 1.0 (§3 of the spec; β ≥ 1 is its mirror on the stall side). [IMPL]

D.1 Discount the emission, not the carried cost

The subtle and important choice is what α multiplies. Multiplying the carried cost would make the horizontal edge weight effectively negative for α < 1, and the table would then "launder" cost by wandering through cheap B-vertices — that is the rejected alternative, and nothing in the shipped code multiplies the carried value. The shipped code scales only the emission charged on a coverage step — literally nw = dv + alpha * cand[w]["E"] (dag_dtw.py:274), with the carried predecessor value dv passed through untouched. For a chain vertex a — a single predecessor p that is itself not a split, outdeg(p) = 1 (the eq (16) case of Appendix A) — the recurrence reads:

(24) D[a][v] = min(   E(a,v) + stepp  (D) advance, full E  ;   β E(a,v) + stallp  (V) stall, βE  ;   α E(a,v) + minv′∈Bpred(v) D[a][v′]  (H) cover, αE  )

(24) is the chain-vertex simplification of the full three-way min of Appendix A / spec §4.1: in general the (D) and (V) branches are sums over all of a's predecessors, each predecessor's contribution scaled by its split factor 1/outdeg(p) — so (24) drops the split factor only because p is not a split, and at a merge the sum runs over every incoming branch, with (V) forcing at least one genuine stall (dag_dtw.py:229, :248). The emission multiplier is the same in every shape, and it is the whole of what α and β touch: a cell entered by A-advance pays the full E, a first match entered by a stall pays βE, and only the continuation of a coverage run is discounted to αE. The extraction (Appendix A, spec §5.2) re-prices every cell from E alone through the identical 1 / β / α ledger, so the discount appears the same way in the forward table and in the cell-level join.

D.2 Coverage-cost derivation

Unroll a single coverage v0→v1→…→vk at a constant drift δ. Vertex v0 is entered by A-advance (full δ); each of the remaining k vertices is entered by a horizontal move (αδ each). The total coverage cost is

(25) δ( 1 + α k ),    k = N−1 = extra covered B-vertices

α = 1 recovers the per-B-point charge δN (spec §4.1: α = β = 1 reproduces the unweighted cost bit-for-bit); the α → 0 limit charges the coverage essentially once (δ, sampling-independent) — a limit only, since the shipped domain is α ∈ (0, 1] (spec §3; the match_dag docstring, dag_dtw.py:1142): at α = 0 a coverage step is free (nw = dv), the cost stops distinguishing run length, and a cover chain no longer strictly descends in D — leaving spec §4.1's pointer-DAG argument resting on its tie-break half alone. The arithmetic is the (H) update nw = dv + α·E inside _fill_row (dag_dtw.py:274); the shipped verification of coverage cost is test_extract_cell_equals_full_space_brute (tests/test_dag_dtw.py:572), which pins the cell join to the full-space brute-force optimum on a dense-B coverage chain at α ∈ {1.0, 0.5, 0.3}.

D.3 No-laundering argument

Because αE ≥ 0 is a non-negative local emission added on every step, the carried cost D is non-decreasing along a coverage run: D[vi] ≥ D[vi−1] for all i — the same non-negativity the code leans on for the (H) relaxation (dag_dtw.py:255–258: "α·E ≥ 0 ⇒ a monotone descent to the unique least fixed point"). α scales the increment, never the carried predecessor value dv — that is the entire argument. Hence the table can never lower its running total by wandering: the pathology that motivated rejecting the carried-cost variant (D.1) is structurally impossible here, at every α in the domain.

D.4 The within-row fixed point

Implementation detail: there is one code path at every α — _fill_row applies alpha unconditionally, with no α = 1 bypass; α = 1 is a property of the recurrence (it reproduces the unweighted cost bit-for-bit), not a branch. Unlike (D)/(V), which read only predecessor rows — final under the §4.0 layer sweep — the (H) term reads other cells of the same row, and B carries no order of its own. So the (H) term is a within-row fixed point, relaxed until a full sweep changes nothing (while changed: …, dag_dtw.py:264–279), lowering D and repointing bpD in the same step so the pointer never desyncs from the value. Convergence follows from the same non-negativity as D.3 (α·E ≥ 0 ⇒ a monotone descent to the unique least fixed point), not from a contraction: it holds at α = 1 and on a cyclic B alike, where any single pass would leave cells un-relaxed (spec §4.1). Because α sits inside the compared candidate value dv + α·E, it lives in the decision cost and shifts which M is chosen — toward more 1:N coverage in dense-target regimes; pinned at α = 0.5 by test_extract_cell_beats_vertex_join_on_divergence, where the optimal matching is a coverage run the vertex-level join misses, and exercised at α ∈ {1.0, 0.5} on jittered reconvergent sources by test_extract_cell_exact_on_dag_diamonds and at weights (1.0, 1.0), (0.7, 1.0), (0.5, 1.5) across the 384-case envelope (scripts/test_dag_point.py). The reported drift, however, stays the raw Σ E of the chosen M (spec §3) — C(M) is the internal comparator (_cost_of, dag_dtw.py:460), while the pipeline surfaces plain geometry as avg_dist_m / drift_m / drift_max_m (spec §11). α is orthogonal to β, to the V3 coupling, and to the extraction machinery.

Appendix E — Emission modes

The emission E(a,v) is the only place the geometry enters the DP, and is identical in graph-DTW and DAG-DTW. Two modes ship.

E.1 Point mode (default) [IMPL]

(26) E(a,v) = ‖a−v‖   (Euclidean, metres)

A B-vertex is scored purely on proximity — point-mode nodes carry no bearing, so the bearing term of (27) never fires (_emit, dag_dtw.py:42–51). The structured 384-case envelope of Appendix G (scripts/test_dag_point.py) is point mode throughout; the full-space brute-force checks, by contrast, are run in point and segment mode with the bearing term active. Point mode's limitation is exactly the nearest-vs-corresponding collapse the earlier conditioning experiments chased (Appendix C): distance alone cannot distinguish two parallel B-edges, so under a large lateral offset the DP prefers the nearer one even when it is the wrong correspondent.

E.2 Segment mode (middle-to-middle + bearing) [IMPL]

Segment mode is a true (A-segment, B-arc)-state DP (_segment_dp_pairs, graph_dtw.py:301). Its state is (i,e) = "A-segment i rides B-arc e = u→v", and its per-state cost is the middle-to-middle distance plus an optional bearing term:

(27) E(i,e) = ‖ mid(ai,ai+1) − mid(u,v) ‖ + λ·dcirc( θ(si), θ(e) )

where mid(·,·) is a segment midpoint, θ a heading, λ = bearing_weight, and dcirc ∈ [0°,180°] the circular heading difference (so a segment and its reverse are 180° apart). The three DP moves become A-advance on the same arc (N:1), arc-advance within a row (Dijkstra, 1:N), or both; the arc-state path is emitted as vertex pairs so all downstream grouping and metrics are shared with point mode.

Bearing convention. θ(e) is the direction of travel along the forward B-arc u→v; θ(si) the direction of the A-segment. Because the local graphs contain forward arcs only and a bidirectional road is two opposing directed edges, a heading difference near 180° signals a wrong-direction (reverse-twin) match and is penalised out.

Why the bearing term, not distance alone. A middle-to-middle distance is blind to a segment rotating about its own middle — two crossing segments sharing a midpoint have zero distance cost. The bearing term is what pins heading, so the recommended working configuration is emission="segment" with λ ∈ [1,5].

E.3 The diamond-fix cost argument [IMPL]

This is the emission-level resolution of the limit the earlier conditioning experiments (Appendix C) ran into. On the reconvergent diamond under lateral shift, point mode collapses both A-branches onto the nearer B-edge because the distance saved by collapsing genuinely exceeds the distance lost — the collapse is the true point-mode optimum, not a search failure. But the correct correspondent branch and the nearer wrong branch differ in heading: collapsing forces the A-segment onto a B-arc whose bearing disagrees, incurring λ·dcirc on every covered segment. For λ large enough that the accumulated bearing penalty exceeds the distance saved, the true split re-emerges as the minimum. This is the honest conclusion of that episode: "the diamond needs the direction term, not a cut" — the direction term changes the objective so that the correct match is genuinely cheapest, whereas conditioning only searched the point-mode objective more exactly.

The direction term is shipped in DAG-DTW, not future work: match_dag(mode="segment", bearing_weight=λ) runs the identical algorithm — layering, recurrence, V3 coupling, cell-level join, the judge — on line_digraph(A) / line_digraph(B), whose nodes are the directed arcs carrying midpoint (x, y) and bearing (dag_dtw.py:121–133), and _emit adds λ·dcirc whenever both nodes carry a bearing (dag_dtw.py:42–51; default bearing_weight = 1.0). Segment mode is verified cross-engine and against full-space brute force on the line graphs with the bearing active. So the shipped resolution of the diamond limit is segment mode.

Historical accuracy. docs/weighted_emission.md §2–§10 develop two earlier variants (endpoint-average distance; bearing charged only on diagonal moves); both were superseded by that document's §11 middle-to-middle form, which is what ships. emission="midpoint" is a deprecated alias for "segment". Only the middle-to-middle form should be cited.

Appendix F — Complexity

Let N be the number of densified A-vertices in the local problem, and VB,EB the vertices and arcs of the local target graph GB. All local graphs are small (a junction neighbourhood), so the polylogarithmic factors are on tiny inputs.

Emission. E(a,v) is O(1) (a distance, plus O(1) bearing in segment mode). The two vertex pools are built by mutual projection, one side each: build_local_digraph (graph_dtw.py:202) gives every B-edge its own nodes plus the projections of A's nodes onto it, and graph_dtw_align builds the A axis from A's nodes plus the projections of B's raw nodes onto A (_node_projection_pool, graph_dtw.py:52). Both are O( (VA+VB)2 ) worst case, typically far less.

Graph-DTW (one A-edge). The DP has N rows; each row's horizontal move is a Dijkstra over GB, O(EB log VB), and vertical/diagonal are O(VB). Total per A-edge:

(28) O( N·EB log VB )

Segment mode multiplies VB by the arc-state factor but stays the same asymptotic form over GB's arcs.

DAG-DTW — the forward table. The order is the longest-path layering of Appendix A (layer_order), an O(VA+EA) sweep plus the tie-break sort — explicitly not a plain topological sort, which does not keep a split's children complete as a group. The table is sparse by the radius gate: an A-vertex's row holds only its candidate cells, so the table is

(29) O( |A| × band ) cells,   band = |cand(a)| (the gate's occupancy)

rather than O(|A|·VB) — absence is the ∞ cell. Filling one cell is a linear scan over Bpred(v) intersected with the row for the advance/stall min: no Dijkstra, no log factor (that heap lives in graph-DTW's row relaxation, eq. 28). Two fixed points sit on top, and both are on the shipped path at every α, including α = 1. (i) The (H) 1:N coverage step reads other cells of the same row, so the row is a within-row fixed point: relax until a full sweep changes nothing (dag_dtw.py:255–279). Because αE ≥ 0 this is a monotone descent to the unique least fixed point — correct even when the row's target subgraph cycles, where a single pass would leave cells un-relaxed (Appendix D). (ii) The V3 coupling (forbid-and-rebuild) rebuilds whole child rows when a split exit dies; the forbidden set only grows, so it reaches its fixed point in at most |cand(split)| rounds per split — rare and bounded, so rows can be filled more than once but not many times. No backward mirror table is built here: backward() is a diagnostic, never on the shipped path.

DAG-DTW — the extraction. extract_cell (dag_dtw.py:705) is one reverse-topological, per-cell sweep over the cell-move graph, preceded by a sink-cell reachability pre-pass (_cell_reachable) that deletes every cell reaching no sink. Coverage runs are implicit — one arc per cover edge, no run cap — and each table is contracted per (entry, pending-signature) key: that contraction is the polynomial bound, and it is what keeps the join a join rather than a cross-product over subtree choices. Merge pendings are discharged at the arms' first common ancestor, so chained merges stay linear; max_rows (default 50 000) raises, never truncates, so a blow-up is loud rather than silent; peak memory is the sweep frontier, since a row is pushed to its readers' inboxes and freed the same turn. There is no traceback, no reconstruction and no re-match step — the winning row's cells map is the relation. Reconvergent sources need no special case: the cell-level join handles them natively and exactly (195/195 against full-space brute force, Appendix G). The honest summary is linear-ish in |A| × band in practice.

The complexity of the retired conditioning solvers is discussed with them, as design history, in Appendix C.

G.3 Validity rules

check_rules(M, src, tgt) (dag_dtw.py:1190) is the judge. It reads the final relation M alone — not a table, not a pointer chain — and returns the violation lists for three rules, each restricted to neighbours present in M:

  1. (V1) Monotonicity — no cross. No A-predecessor of a matched vertex may sit on a B-successor of that vertex's cell: a backward step is forbidden.
  2. (V2) Every cell is fed. Each matched cell either continues its own vertex's run, or every A-predecessor feeds it — which is what keeps a merge from being entered at two different points.
  3. (V3) Every cell continues. The mirror of (V2) over the successors — what keeps a split from being left at two different points.

The harness checks (V4) full coverage alongside (every A-vertex appears in M; a source's entry and a sink's exit are free, interior junctions pinned). Together these four are the correctness criterion: they are properties of M, so the same judge grades every engine, both modes, and any hand-built relation. And they are enforced, not merely reported — the extraction tries its rows cheapest-first and returns the first one the judge passes, so an invalid matching is never returned: the call either yields a valid M or raises. Across the 384-case envelope that is 384/384 valid returns and 0 invalid outputs.

  • Tiny dense-B cases vs full enumeration, in point and segment mode (bearing term active): the cell-level join equals the brute-force optimum to the digit.
  • Random mini polytrees vs full-space brute: cell join exact and valid, every case.
  • Reconvergent DAG sources — jittered subdivided diamonds and random subdivided reconvergent DAGs: cell join exact against the full-space optimum, all valid; 195/195 over the standing set (docs/dag_dtw_matching.md §10.2), 42/42 over the reduced version pinned in the suite (12 diamonds + 30 random DAGs). This is the structure the tree argument does not cover.
  • The pinned divergence case: the cell join strictly beats the vertex-level junction join and equals the full-space optimum. The vertex join is exact only over the stored-history family — the optimal coverage run there is one the forward pointers never stored — which is precisely why the cell-level join, and not it, is the extraction of record.

G.6 Stated limits (methodology honesty)

  • Synthetic-only. Every figure above is on hand-built structures — the four envelope trees, jittered diamonds, random reconvergent DAGs — with coordinates written directly in metres. They validate the algorithm's correctness and exactness (does it return a valid matching; is that matching the optimum), not its matching accuracy on real OSM↔NVDB data.
  • Point mode is shift-sensitive on reconvergent geometry. With no bearing term, a large enough rigid offset makes the nearest target edge stop being the corresponding one, and two arms of a reconvergence can prefer the same side. This is a limit of the point-mode emission, not of the extraction — and the shipped answer is segment mode (Appendix E): the same algorithm on the line digraphs, where the state is a segment pair and the bearing term separates the arms. It is implemented and brute-force-verified with the bearing active, not future work.
  • The two engines can diverge — both still valid. The vertex-level junction join is exact only over the stored-history family; it diverges from the cell-level join in dense-target coverage regimes, where the optimal run is one the forward pointers never stored. Both return valid matchings, and C(cell) ≤ C(vertex join) is pinned in the suite — a violation would be an exactness bug by definition. The vertex join is a cross-check, never the engine of record.
  • The validator limit on a cyclic B. On a 2-cycle p⇄q the local (V1) predicate cannot orient a step, so every matching that separates the two source vertices — including the geometrically correct one, and its mirror — is flagged. This is a property of the predicate, not of the matcher: the cell join refuses loudly rather than returning something invalid, the vertex join returns the valid stall, and engine="all" still succeeds. Pinned by test, documented, unfixed.
  • Feasibility failures are loud, never silent. An emptied split, an unreachable vertex, a vertex with no surviving cell, an all-invalid root join — each raises a ValueError naming the vertex and telling the caller to increase match_radius_m. Candidates are radius-gated (match_radius_m, with a k_min nearest-neighbour floor), so a too-tight gate is a located, honest failure rather than a quietly wrong match.
